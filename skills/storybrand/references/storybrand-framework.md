# StoryBrand framework reference

Practical summary of StoryBrand's public framework material. This is a method guide, not copied source text. Use it to structure messaging; do not treat it as evidence for user, product, customer, outcome, or authority claims.

## Core idea: clarity before cleverness

Customers give limited attention to unfamiliar marketing. Make message low-calorie: low cognitive effort to grasp on first read. Lead with a clear customer goal, problem blocking it, and simple action path.

StoryBrand applies the hero's-journey pattern to messaging: the customer or client is **the hero of their own story**. They should recognise themselves and their quest immediately. The organisation is a critical guide who helps the hero succeed; it does not compete for the limelight by presenting itself as the hero. This customer-as-hero / organisation-as-guide relationship is the central positioning rule of the framework.

Use one dominant message and action per content unit unless user has a confirmed reason to present alternatives. Remove details that do not help a customer understand:

- what they want,
- what blocks them,
- how offer helps,
- what to do next,
- why action matters.

Prefer familiar words, short sentences, concrete outcomes, and a visible CTA. Test: can intended customer say what offer is, why it matters, and next step after one quick read? If not, reduce cognitive load before adding persuasion.

## Script scope

Choose one scope before filling the BrandScript: a brand, division, offer, campaign, or other coherent decision context. Give that scope one primary customer desire. Create separate scripts when audiences, offers, or desired outcomes materially differ; do not force competing heroes or quests into one story.

## Seven-part BrandScript template

1. **Character / hero** — the customer or client is **the hero of their own story**, not the organisation.
   - **Who is this hero?** Name one clearly defined customer or customer type. They should recognise themselves immediately: “That’s me.” Avoid broad audiences, internal segments, or several competing heroes.
   - **What is their quest?** State one specific, tangible goal the hero wants to achieve. It should be an outcome they would actively seek, not a feature of the offer or a vague aspiration.

2. **Problem** — identify the obstacle preventing the character from reaching that goal.
   - **Villain / root cause (optional):** What named root cause focuses the problem? It can be a circumstance, system, or friction—not a fabricated enemy or claim about a competitor.
   - **External problem:** What practical situation, task, barrier, or missing capability is in the way? Describe the visible problem, not the solution.
   - **Internal problem:** How does that external problem make the customer feel? Name a credible frustration, fear, uncertainty, or cost to confidence.
   - **Philosophical problem:** Why should this situation not be acceptable? Express the value or principle at stake without exaggeration.
   - Keep the three levels connected: the external situation causes the internal experience, which makes the philosophical statement meaningful.

3. **Guide** — the organisation helps the character rather than competing to be the hero.
   - **How does the organisation serve the journey?** Position it as the guide that equips or helps the hero reach that goal. Never frame the brand as the hero whose achievement deserves the customer's attention.
   - **Empathy:** Show that the guide understands the customer’s problem and why it matters. Use customer-centred language and a believable acknowledgement of their experience.
   - **Authority:** Give the customer a reason to trust the guide: relevant evidence, experience, process, credential, or proof. If the evidence is absent, record an evidence gap rather than inventing authority.

4. **Plan** — reduce uncertainty by showing a short, low-friction path forward.
   - **Process plan:** What does the customer do first, what happens next, and what do they receive or achieve? Use a short customer-facing path; three action-led steps is the default. If the real process is longer, group it honestly into a few phases.
   - **Agreement plan (optional):** What evidence-backed promise, policy, standard, or guarantee reduces a relevant fear? Include only commitments the organisation can honour.
   - **Paradigm plan (optional):** Is there a simple, supported change in how the customer should understand the problem before they act? Use this only when it genuinely reduces confusion.

5. **Call to action** — tell the customer exactly what to do next.
   - **Direct CTA:** State the primary conversion action plainly, such as buy, book, request, or start. Make the commitment honest and proportional to the ask.
   - **Transitional CTA:** Offer a genuine lower-commitment step, such as a guide, checklist, demo, sample, or assessment, for customers who are not ready for the direct action.

6. **Failure stakes** — show the credible cost of inaction.
   - **What continues or worsens if the customer does nothing?** Name a realistic practical or emotional consequence linked to the stated problem.
   - Keep stakes proportionate. Do not fabricate urgency, fear, loss, or consequences the evidence cannot support.

7. **Success outcome** — show the customer’s specific, better future after acting.
   - **What has changed for the customer?** Describe the tangible outcome of completing the plan or using the offer.
   - **How does that outcome feel or enable their goal?** Keep the benefit customer-centred, concrete, and supported rather than making an inflated promise.
   - **Character transformation (optional):** Express the supported change as `from <current state> → to <better state>`. Do not turn a hoped-for feeling or outcome into a promise without evidence.

## One-liner

Use the compact **Problem → Solution → Reward** pattern to give a repeatable answer to “what do you do?”

1. Name the customer inside a specific problem.
2. Say how the offer provides a plain, differentiating solution.
3. End with the supported reward or better state.

Test it aloud and with someone unfamiliar with the offer. They should be able to identify the audience, offer, value, and next logical question. Use the resulting BrandScript as source material for channel copy and talking points, not as finished copy or a literal narrative.

## Workshop rules

- Start with the chosen script scope, supplied offer, audience, desired action, evidence, constraints, and customer language.
- Separate `Confirmed fact`, `Assumption`, and `Question`. An empty field is not permission to make something up.
- Keep external, internal, and philosophical problems distinct. External is situation; internal is felt effect; philosophical is value judgement.
- Make plan actionable and short. A process description is not a plan until customer can see their next steps.
- Use direct CTAs without hiding required commitment. Transitional CTA must offer genuine value, not a disguised sales action.
- Prefer simple words, concrete outcomes, active verbs, and customer language over internal jargon.
- Keep proof tied to source. Testimonials, metrics, awards, certifications, and comparisons need user-provided evidence.
- Do not claim StoryBrand certification, affiliation, endorsement, or outcomes caused by framework.

## Optional local SB7 clarity score

This is a local diagnostic checklist, not an official StoryBrand score and not proof that copy will convert. Award one point for each confirmed item (maximum 10).

### SB7: 7 points

1. Customer is clear main character with one concrete desired outcome.
2. External, internal, and philosophical problem are distinct and fit supplied evidence.
3. Organisation is guide with grounded empathy and supported authority.
4. Plan gives customer short, actionable path.
5. Direct CTA is visible, specific, and honest about commitment.
6. Failure stakes are credible; no fabricated urgency or fear.
7. Success is specific, customer-centred, and supported.

### One-liner: 3 points

1. Names customer problem.
2. Says how offer helps and resulting outcome.
3. Can be understood and repeated after one quick read.

Report score with missing items only when user asks for a scored review. Never treat score as validation of market, claims, accessibility, legal compliance, or conversion performance.

## Content-alignment review

When reviewing or generating content against a BrandScript, check:

1. Customer is recognisable and remains main character.
2. Desired outcome appears before brand detail.
3. Problem is concrete; internal/philosophical framing fits evidence and audience.
4. Brand speaks as guide through empathy and supported authority, not as hero.
5. Plan reduces uncertainty with clear steps.
6. CTA is visible, specific, and proportional to user intent.
7. Stakes and success are credible, customer-centred, and not overclaimed.
8. Claims, proof, terminology, and promised outcomes match script's confirmed facts.

Report each mismatch with content location, relevant script element, evidence, and proposed revision. For generation, preserve confirmed facts; label additions requiring evidence.

## Illustrative example

Illustration only — not a claim template:

1. **Character / hero** — the customer is the hero, not the proposal tool.
   - **Who is this hero?** An independent consultant preparing proposals for prospective clients.
   - **What is their quest?** Send a clear, tailored proposal faster so they can follow up while an opportunity is still active.

2. **Problem** — scattered proposal information makes the hero's quest harder.
   - **Villain / root cause (optional):** `Question` — is the root cause scattered documents, an inconsistent sales process, or something else supported by customer evidence?
   - **External problem:** Service details, pricing, and prior proposal material live across several documents.
   - **Internal problem:** They feel disorganised and less confident before a client call.
   - **Philosophical problem:** Capable professionals should not lose work to avoidable administrative friction.
   - **Connected problem:** Searching and rebuilding proposal details creates stress and delays follow-up.

3. **Guide** — the tool helps the consultant move forward.
   - **How does the organisation serve the journey?** The proposal tool acts as a guide that helps the hero organise their service details and create the proposal; it does not claim the spotlight as the hero.
   - **Empathy:** “You should not need to rebuild the same proposal every time.”
   - **Authority:** `Question` — what evidence shows that the tool helps consultants prepare proposals faster or more consistently?

4. **Plan** — a short path from details to a sendable proposal.
   - **Process plan:** 1. Add service details and pricing. 2. Choose and tailor a proposal template. 3. Review and send the proposal.
   - **Agreement plan (optional):** `Question` — what support, data handling, cancellation, or quality commitment is approved and relevant to the consultant's concern?
   - **Paradigm plan (optional):** `Assumption` — a reusable proposal process can reduce the need to start from a blank document each time.

5. **Call to action** — invite an appropriate next step.
   - **Direct CTA:** “Start a proposal.”
   - **Transitional CTA:** “See a sample proposal.”

6. **Failure stakes** — show the credible cost of remaining stuck.
   - **What continues or worsens if the customer does nothing?** `Assumption` — manual rebuilding continues to slow follow-up and leave the consultant feeling disorganised.

7. **Success outcome** — show the better future for the hero.
   - **What has changed for the customer?** They can prepare and send a consistent proposal with less preparation.
   - **How does that outcome feel or enable their goal?** `Question` — what customer evidence supports the resulting confidence, saved time, or improved follow-up?
   - **Character transformation (optional):** `Assumption` — from feeling disorganised before a client call → to feeling prepared to send a tailored proposal.
