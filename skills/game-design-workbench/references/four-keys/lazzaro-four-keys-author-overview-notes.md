## What overview establishes

Author's current public labels are **Hard Fun, Easy Fun, Serious Fun, and People Fun**. Page associates Hard Fun with fiero, Easy Fun with curiosity, Serious Fun with relaxation and excitement, and People Fun with amusement. These are focal associations, not proof that each key produces exactly one emotion.

Overview positions emotion as a product of interaction. It contrasts this interest with market-research responses about surface features and with usability work aimed mainly at reducing frustration. Its use of Four Keys extends beyond games to motivation in other interactive software. That extension is an author claim; no separate supporting study is supplied on this page.

Page also proposes that players move among keys within a session and attributes at least three keys to bestselling games. It does not expose a sampling procedure, comparison group, or causal test for the bestseller statement. Treat it as framework guidance from its author, not a rule predicting sales or a quota for this project.

## Terminology bridge to original research

[2004 report notes](lazzaro-2004-why-we-play-games-notes.md) preserve original names: Internal Experience, Hard Fun, Easy Fun, and Other Players. Later overview uses Serious Fun and People Fun for the corresponding places in its four-part account. This is a vocabulary bridge across inspected sources, not proof that every later definition is identical to its 2004 formulation.

Serious Fun should not automatically mean solemn subject matter. This overview explicitly includes relaxation and excitement. Earlier report includes self-appraisal, changing mood, and perceived personal value. Together these sources support a broader reading than either “educational games” or “important story themes” alone.

People Fun should not automatically mean online multiplayer mechanics. Earlier report includes spectatorship, teaching, friendship, and talk surrounding play. Later overview's emphasis on amusement does not erase that wider social context.
