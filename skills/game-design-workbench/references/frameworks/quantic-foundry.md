## Scope

Compare an intended experience with activities that might support interests described by the published multidimensional motivation vocabulary.

## Inputs and query support

Roles: `aesthetics`, `mechanics`, `dynamics`, `challenges`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

## Working concepts

Vocabulary: Action: Destruction/Excitement; Social: Competition/Community; Mastery: Challenge/Strategy; Achievement: Completion/Power; Creativity: Discovery/Design; Immersion: Fantasy/Story. These are attributed model labels, not workbench scores.

- **Motivation vocabulary:** The published model offers several dimensions of interest rather than one exclusive player identity. Use its attributed labels to clarify intended activities, not to assign an unmeasured preference profile.
- **Activity and appeal:** An interest-support claim should name what the player repeatedly does. A fantasy theme, completion counter, or strategic label does not by itself establish immersion, achievement appeal, or mastery appeal.
- **Overlap and tension:** One mechanic can support several interests under different choices. Supporting one interest can also constrain another; identify the actual rule relationship rather than assuming categories are independent feature bins.
- **Version and evidence boundary:** This pack retains vocabulary from the cited published explanation. It does not claim current questionnaire access, reproduce scoring, or certify that any particular game attracts a measured audience.

## Apply to selected slice

1. Start with an interest or experience intention supplied by the designer. If none exists, offer a small optional vocabulary choice rather than classifying the audience automatically.
2. Select relevant published labels and explain their intended local meaning through activities. Read mechanics, dynamics, challenges, and evidence. Keep the full vocabulary available without reviewing every dimension.
3. Trace one interest to repeated choices and consequences. Ask what activity, rather than theme or marketing language, supports the connection. Mark the connection an interpretation until evidence supports it.
4. Examine overlap, conflict, and deliberate exclusions. Compare a plausible alternative appeal of the same activity. Do not add features merely to cover every motivation group.
5. Return an activity-to-interest hypothesis, its boundary, and a useful disconfirming observation. No survey reconstruction, audience dataset, preference percentage, personality inference, or single player-type assignment follows.

## Original prompts

1. Which named interest is actually part of the current design intention?
2. What repeated player activity supports that interest rather than merely mentioning it in theme or copy?
3. Could the same mechanic serve different interests through different choices?
4. What tension appears when supporting two intended interests at once?
5. Which interests are deliberately outside scope, with no need to add corresponding features?
6. What play evidence would distinguish the intended appeal from the designer’s preferred interpretation?

## Worked example

- **Authored facts:** The puzzle emphasizes deliberate planning and constrained crate placement. No surveyed profile or observed motivation is supplied.
- **Interpretation:** Reasoning about placement can plausibly support Challenge/Strategy interests in the cited vocabulary. This is a statement about intended activity, not a measured description of puzzle players.
- **Counterreading:** Someone may engage chiefly with completion or curiosity about a solution. The same visible action does not uniquely identify their motivation.
- **Useful next question:** Which activity is meant to sustain interest after the first solution, and what voluntary account or observed choice could distinguish the intended appeal from a competing explanation?

## Evidence and counterchecks

- Keep an individual account, a design intention, and a population claim separate. One enthusiastic participant cannot validate an audience segment.
- Observed choices may support several interpretations. Repeated retries could reflect challenge interest, completion goals, external pressure, or confusion.
- Any later audience research needs its own authorized method and provenance. Published model labels do not supply missing questionnaire results or local validation.

## Common traps

- Do not turn dimensions into mutually exclusive identities or call one person a single motivation type.
- Do not infer appeal from theme alone or treat every published label as a required feature.
- Do not recreate proprietary questionnaire wording, scoring, or empirical claims from unavailable source material.

## Missing data and evidence limits

Discuss intended interests without surveyed profiles. If no interest is supplied, offer vocabulary as optional hypotheses rather than assigning an audience.

No questionnaire, preference scoring, cohort collection, audience dataset, personality inference, or single player type. Published empirical model does not validate a workbench-specific support claim.
