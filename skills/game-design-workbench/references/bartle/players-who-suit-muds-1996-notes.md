## Origin and evidence strength

The model began with discussion among experienced participants of one commercial UK MUD, November 1989–May 1990. Approximately 15 people contributed heavily and another 15 intermittently. Bartle, the senior administrator, organized their stated interests into four recurring approaches. This is a local, expert-participant account, not a random population survey or validated personality instrument. [Source: “A Simple Taxonomy.”]

The source explicitly excludes some reasons for participation, such as learning programming through administrative facilities. It argues the taxonomy can describe social MUDs too, while warning that its population-management advice transfers less readily to social or heavily role-playing settings. Bartle also explicitly disclaims a conventionally rigorous psychological treatment. [Source: “A Simple Taxonomy”; endnote 1.]

## Conceptual structure

Two questions organize the model: whether interest centers on other participants or the simulated world, and whether engagement emphasizes imposing effects or responsive involvement. Their intersections distinguish four approaches:

- Achievement: pursue goals and recognized progress within the game.
- Exploration: investigate places, rules, and hidden behavior; not merely reveal map locations.
- Socialising: develop relationships and converse with actual participants.
- Killing: exert power over other participants. In the article’s historical examples, unwanted interference and distress matter more than the mere presence of combat.

These are orientations toward play, not four mutually exclusive lists of actions. The same action can serve different ends: combat can support progress, experimentation, revenge, or domination. The article allows overlap and changes with mood, although Bartle also proposes that many participants have a dominant preference. Removing that latter claim would misrepresent the original. [Source: “A Simple Taxonomy”; “Interest Graph.”]

The distinction between a human target and a computer-controlled creature is consequential. Fighting a monster does not by itself demonstrate an interest in imposing on people. Likewise, the article describes combat-oriented settings in which frequent deaths have little emotional cost; it does not treat those as equivalent to its central account of killers. [Source: “Interest Graph”; “Killers v. Killers.”]

## Beyond the familiar four labels

Much of the article concerns relationships among approaches, rather than classification alone. A feature can benefit one interest while discouraging another. Bartle explores how communication facilities, world size, reward structures, command depth, and building permissions might shift participation. These are historically situated proposals, not tested prescriptions for every multiplayer game. [Source: “Changing the Player Type Balance.”]

The article distinguishes direct effects from secondary feedback. For example, harassment can drive away people who provide a community’s conversation and relationships; declining participation then changes the environment for others. Its detailed case descriptions concern stereotypes and expressly must not be assumed true of a particular individual. [Source: “Player Interactions.”]

Bartle’s equilibrium is not equal headcounts. It concerns relatively stable relationships among participating approaches. The desired atmosphere is an administrator’s choice. The source discusses multiple stable configurations, including social-dominated and mixed communities, as well as collapse. It does not prescribe a universal four-way percentage split. [Source: “Changing the Player Type Balance”; “Dynamics.”]

The model’s proposed feedback links omit outside influences and capacity constraints. Bartle acknowledges that enforced role-play or other external structures could stabilize arrangements his simplified account would not predict. Consequently, the population arrows are hypotheses within a bounded model, not measured causal laws. [Source: “Player Interactions”; “Dynamics.”]

## Reading cautions

- Do not collapse exploration into sightseeing, achievement into all success, or killing into all competition.
- Do not infer an individual’s motive from one visible action.
- Do not treat the article’s sharp stereotypes or dated social generalizations as validated facts about present-day people.
- The text discusses harmful conduct as part of community dynamics. That is not evidence that harassment is necessary or desirable.
- The model addresses shared virtual worlds. It does not establish usefulness for a solo puzzle merely because the puzzle has goals or objects.
- These notes are research evidence about Bartle’s argument. They provide no evidence about this repository’s example game or its players.
