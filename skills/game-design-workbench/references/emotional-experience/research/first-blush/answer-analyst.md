# Emotional-experience vocabulary: answer-analyst orientation

**Status:** first-blush scholarly orientation; provisional suggestions, not final synthesis or publication-ready assertions. Reviewed 2026-10-21. Own definitions below require human claim-fidelity review before reuse.

## Result and boundary

Accessible sources support a substantial, non-exhaustive vocabulary without adopting their taxonomies. Lazzaro supplies game-specific distinctions, especially effortful triumph, mentor pride, and pleasure in a rival's misfortune. An accessible account of OCC supplies useful contrasts between consequences, prospects, actions, and other people's fortunes. Desmet supplies design-oriented positive-emotion distinctions and separates the thing encountered from what a feeling concerns. Scherer and Smith–Ellsworth provide important cautions about definitions, related states, and the limits of appraisal evidence.

The candidate section contains 42 entries, including four explicitly scope-limited affective-state suggestions. Some entries overlap; these are not 42 demonstrated independent categories. Count is not an evidence standard. Important requested terms remain gaps rather than being padded with unsupported definitions.

This is **not** a nineteenth framework, emotion-scoring system, universal taxonomy, instrument adaptation, or measured-neurochemistry account. Existing 18 guides remain selected-only. Nothing here requires categories, record fields, a schema, or applying any guide. No changes to those guides, Schell expansion, or other workers' records were made.

Working language:

- **Experiencer:** person feeling something. Player, observer, character, and designer are not interchangeable experiencers.
- **Target:** who or what the feeling concerns. Not a demographic target audience.
- **Source/elicitor:** meaningful situation or appraisal associated with the feeling. Not an asserted biological cause, necessary cognitive mechanism, or guarantee that a design produces it.
- **Definition:** original short wording proposed here, not a quotation, validated operationalization, or authorized replacement for an author's definition.
- **Support:** source gives relevant meaning, contrast, or situated example. Support varies; row citations do not make every extension established fact.

Coverage questions: Which distinctions clarify achievement and setbacks? Which separate self-evaluation, other-directed feelings, and relationships? Which concern threat, discovery, pleasure, or changed expectations? Which labels instead name expression, absorption, motivation, mood, or disposition?

Selected research guidance: AI-assisted research, because interpretation must remain traceable and provisional; historical/archival research, because terminology and exact primary-text meaning matter. No attribution-history investigation was attempted.

## Bounded evidence anchors

Quotes below are exact short extracts from inspected text, with conversion limitations above. Surrounding explanations are paraphrase or interpretation, not quotations. These anchors support meaning contrasts; they do not establish universal mechanisms. Row locators in the candidate section provide narrower checking points where one anchor covers several terms.

### L anchors: game-specific distinctions

- **L1 — “Emotions During Play,” Fiero row:** “Personal triumph over adversity.” Same row: “They do not need to experience anger prior to success, but it does require effort.” Supports effortful accomplishment, not necessary frustration or repeated failure. §2c discusses success and relief together; co-occurrence does not make them synonyms.
- **L2 — same table, Naches/Kvell row:** “Pleasure or pride at the accomplishment of a child or mentee.” Kvell passage: “express this pride in one's child or mentee to others.” §4d explicitly situates naches in a mentor watching someone succeed.
- **L3 — same table, Schadenfreude row:** “Gloat over misfortune of a rival”. Supports this report's rival-focused use; not all reactions to another person's success or failure.
- **L4 — §2a table:** anger/frustration: “Opposition to an important goal”; boredom: “Lack of interest in the outcome or in playing”. Report combines anger and frustration here; it does not establish a strict boundary between them.
- **L5 — §3a table:** wonder: “Over whelming improbability.” Awe: “Combination of wonder with a fear and dread.” Preserve the source's narrower usage; do not universalize the requirement of fear for awe.
- **L6 — §4a table, Amusement row:** “Something that's funny”. “Emotions During Play,” Surprise row: “Sudden change”. These are the report's brief cues, not sufficient conditions for observed emotion.

### O anchors: consequences, actions, and their combinations

- **O1 — §2/Table 1, PDF p. 3:** joy concerns “a desirable event”; distress “an undesirable event”. §3(1), pp. 3–4 clarifies that event language concerns appraised consequences, not intrinsic valence of an event.
- **O2 — Table 1, Relief and Disappointment rows, p. 3:** relief concerns “the disconfirmation of the prospect of an undesirable event”; disappointment concerns “the disconfirmation of the prospect of a desirable event”. Table 1 also contrasts hoped-for prospects with satisfaction when a desirable prospect is confirmed.
- **O3 — Table 1, Pride and Admiration rows, p. 3:** “one’s own praiseworthy action” versus “someone else’s praiseworthy action”. Shame/reproach use the corresponding blameworthy-action contrast. These are this account's action-focused specifications, not complete everyday meanings of the words.
- **O4 — Table 1, Gratitude row, p. 3:** “someone else’s praiseworthy action” and “the related desirable event”. Remorse combines one's own blameworthy action with its related undesirable event. This supports a consequence-plus-agency distinction; not a claim that every everyday instance requires consciously computing a conjunction.
- **O5 — Table 1, fortunes-of-others rows, p. 3:** “presumed to be desirable for someone else” and “presumed to be undesirable for someone else”. The person's own pleasure/displeasure changes the term. These presumed outcomes are not objective proof of what benefited or harmed the other person.
- **O6 — §2 Fear specification, PDF p. 2:** token list includes “anxious”. §3(1), p. 4 says prospect includes “both future events and uncertain (past or current) events”. This source cannot validate an anxiety/fear split based simply on future versus present.

### D anchors: positive distinctions and target/source separation

- **D1 — “Stage 2: Clustering Emotion Words under Emotion Types,” paragraph beginning “The underlying appraisal…”:** sympathy and compassion are grouped around “suffering, distress, or misfortune of another person” and “the wish to relieve the suffering”. This supports overlap here, not a newly asserted distinction between those words.
- **D2 — same paragraph:** confidence involves “one’s powers or of reliance on one’s circumstances”. Courage involves “withstand danger, fear, or difficulty”; the author explicitly distinguishes it from awareness of one's capacities.
- **D3 — paragraph beginning “The componential approach…”:** pride family shares “an enjoyable sense of self-worth or achievement”. Triumph is included in this family. The broad family does not erase Lazzaro's narrower fiero usage.
- **D4 — “Six Sources…,” “(2) Meaning-focus”:** “in this case the object of the emotion is the designer”. This is the innovative-product/admired-designer example. Product stimulus and target need not be identical.
- **D5 — “(4) Activity-focus”:** “The emotion is not directed toward the product”. The sailing example subsequently distinguishes desire for the boat from anticipated pleasurable sailing. These are situated examples, not necessary conditions for all desire.
- **D6 — “Typology of Positive Emotions,” opening paragraph:** worship includes “idolize, honour, and be devoted to someone”. This one explicit body definition was inspected; the full figure was not.
- **D7 — “General Discussion,” paragraph beginning “Why should we invest…”:** fascination involves “increasing knowledge of the target of interest”; contentment involves “savour their current life circumstances and recent successes”. These describe tendencies, so deriving short experience definitions from them remains interpretive.
- **D8 — “General Discussion,” paragraph beginning “Note that some…”:** “energized, relaxation, and dreaminess” can describe moods; “courage, confidence, and kindness” can describe traits. The author also uses them for situated responses. This does not settle their classification for every use.

### S/E/G anchors: limits that matter

- **S1 — “Exploring the semantic space…,” PDF p. 13, printed p. 707:** “How many emotions are there? I submit that there is currently no answer to this question.” This is Scherer's position, not a timeless proof about the field.
- **S2 — “How can emotions be measured?”, PDF p. 15, printed p. 709:** “there is no single gold-standard method for its measurement”. Read in context of his component-process definition, not as rejection of all measurement.
- **E1 — “Properties of the Emotions—Shame and guilt,” printed p. 833 / PDF p. 21:** “virtually indistinguishable from each other”; “a sense of self-blame is central”. This particular study did not establish a clean shame/guilt boundary.
- **E2 — “Properties of the Emotions—Interest and hope,” p. 832 / PDF p. 20:** interest descriptions involved wanting “to learn something about the situation”. Fear discussion, p. 834 / PDF p. 22: “uncertainty about whether or not one will be able to escape or avoid an unpleasant outcome”. Uncertainty is not exclusive to anxiety or confusion.
- **E3 — “Future Directions,” p. 836 / PDF p. 24:** “The nature of this role needs to be further examined.” Authors discuss the unresolved relation between cognition and emotion. Methodological reading here: recalled appraisal associations do not establish cognitive causes; that limitation is not a quotation from an explicit causal experiment.
- **G1 — “Format”:** “a single emotion or a blend of several emotions”. Instrument permits mixed reports, but does not supply the exact definitions or causal routes proposed in a new vocabulary.

## Requested contrasts: what can and cannot be said now

1. **Fiero versus relief; relief versus triumph.** L1 supports earned personal triumph; O2/L §1a support avoidance/release from something unwanted. A player can feel relief because a teammate rescued them, with no personal overcoming. A player can feel triumph and relief together. Fiero does not require prior anger, frustration, repeated failure, or a raised-arm display. “Triumph” is a broad neighboring label, not an additional proven category or an exact cross-language synonym in every use.
2. **Naches versus personal pride.** L2 concerns a child/mentee's accomplishment; O3/D3 concern favorable self-evaluation. Mentor's personal pride in teaching well and naches at the learner's success can coexist. The target distinction is useful even when one event affords both. Happy-for covers a less relationship-specific response to another's good fortune.
3. **Gratitude versus admiration; pride versus gratitude.** O3/O4 distinguish approval of another's action from approval plus a desirable consequence linked to it. D's admired-designer example shows how a visible object can point to a person as target. Pride concerns favorable self-evaluation; gratitude turns toward another's welcome contribution. A rescue animation does not prove gratitude: it might invite admiration for skill, pride in teamwork, relief, annoyance, or no reported feeling.
4. **Remorse versus shame.** In the accessible OCC account, shame is disapproval of one's own blameworthy action; remorse also includes displeasure about its consequence. Do not attribute the popular “whole self versus specific act” rule to this passage. E1 also cautions that shame/guilt were not cleanly separated in that study. Remorse, shame, guilt, and regret need not be exclusive labels.
5. **Envy versus schadenfreude — one side remains a gap.** L3 supports pleasure at another's setback. A useful **unverified working contrast** would put envy around another's advantage and one's own wanting/comparison, rather than their misfortune. But O's displeasure-at-another's-good-fortune row is resentment, not a verified definition of envy. S Table 4 merely lists envy word stems. No full envy entry is promoted here. Need direct definition/appraisal support before adopting the proposed comparison.

## Why named sources do not validate new target/elicitor rows

A bibliography names a research neighborhood; it does not license any plausible sentence under that name. Before promoting a row, review four separate links:

1. **Term to meaning:** Does the inspected passage explain this sense, or merely contain the word? G's wheel labels, S's coding stems, and C's reported category membership are not exact definitions.
2. **Meaning to target:** Does the source support who/what the feeling concerns? D4/D5 show why “the game/object caused it” cannot substitute for target. A gifted artifact may direct feeling toward the giver, one's history, or the activity it enables.
3. **Meaning to eliciting reading:** Does the passage specify expectation, agency, effort, another's fortunes, or some other relationship? Newly adding unfairness, personal responsibility, rivalry, training, or uncertainty requires support. O's broad gratitude specification, for example, does not establish necessary indebtedness or repayment.
4. **Source account to proposed play situation:** Is this a source assertion, an original model definition, a recalled participant report, or an analyst's design inference? A designer's intention and a hypothetical play example are not observations. None proves a biological/cognitive cause, reliable emotional response, cultural universality, or population prevalence.

Different sources also partition language differently. D clusters compassion/sympathy and includes triumph within pride; L distinguishes naches and fiero for game discussion; O uses narrowly specified resentment and shame; E did not differentiate some neighboring labels. Preserve these differences instead of presenting their union as a validated master taxonomy.

A source's action tendency is not a guaranteed behavior or the complete meaning of the emotion. Fascination's exploration tendency does not prove that every exploration action is fascination. Reproach can name internal disapproval or a spoken expression; a row must state which use it means.
