# Emotions and felt experiences — working vocabulary

A substantial, **non-exhaustive** vocabulary for describing possible player, helper, rival, spectator, or character experiences. Use ordinary language, an entry here, or another supplied term. A word does not need dictionary recognition to express a precise intention. Preserve the user's meaning rather than force a match.

Read the [framing procedure](../../workflows/emotional-experience.md) when developing an emotional target or sequence. This reference is neither a nineteenth framework nor a validated taxonomy, questionnaire, diagnostic tool, or design score. Groups below are reading aids; no design must cover them.

## How to read entries

**All definitions are original working wording**, not copied dictionary entries or official instrument items. They offer a discussable sense, not the only valid meaning. Source accounts sometimes disagree or group words differently. A user can mean a broader, narrower, personal, or culturally specific sense; make that sense explicit.

- **Target:** who/what the feeling concerns, not a demographic target audience. It can be a person, action, outcome, possibility, memory, object, or one's present condition. Some states have no clear object.
- **Possible elicitor/appraisal:** an illustrative meaningful situation or interpretation. It is not a proven biological cause, exclusive trigger, guaranteed effect, or required mechanic. Context and prior state can change the response.
- **Basis:** a meaning/usage source or an explicit editorial convention. Bibliographic source and eliciting source are different things. The [source index](README.md#source-keys) gives inspected passages, authority, access, and limits.
- **EDITORIAL:** an independently proposed ordinary-language distinction or extension, not a definition established by the cited research. Treat it as a working agreement, not a deficient or invalid feeling. USER identifies supplied meaning, not published empirical evidence.

Source keys: [DESMET](README.md#desmet), [LAZZARO](README.md#lazzaro), [OCC-REVIEW](README.md#occ-review), [SMITH-ELLSWORTH](README.md#smith-ellsworth), [NOBA](README.md#noba), [EDITORIAL](README.md#editorial), [USER](README.md#user).

Table senses can overlap; intensity labels do not create measured thresholds. An intended emotion is not evidence that anyone felt it. Distinguish the real person's experience from an avatar's performance or a designer's interpretation.

| Relief | Ease or pleasure when an unwanted burden recedes or feared outcome is averted. | Released strain, avoided harm, or ended uncertainty. | Safety, assistance, or completion removes something troubling; personal mastery is not required. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1; LAZZARO §1a; DESMET Figure 1. |

| Regret | Wishing a past action, omission, or outcome had been otherwise. | Past choice/event and an imagined alternative. | A different path now seems preferable. Moral wrongdoing is not required in this working sense. | EDITORIAL; not an inspected general definition from OCC-REVIEW. |
| Scheißenbedauer | Immediate regret after sending a message one wishes had not been sent. | Own just-sent communication. | The send action is followed by an unwanted realization. Broader action-regret uses need explicit agreement. | USER; supplied usage, no origin or dictionary claim. |

## Anticipation, uncertainty, and threat

| Term | Working definition | Target | Possible elicitor/appraisal | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hope | Positive anticipation of a valued possibility that remains unsettled. | Desired possible outcome. | Success seems possible but not assured; hope need not imply confidence or control. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1; DESMET Figure 1. |
| Anticipation | Looking forward to an expected occurrence or encounter. | Upcoming event or experience. | Preparing for something expected. DESMET foregrounds a desirable event; neutral/unpleasant anticipation is a broader working use. | DESMET Figure 1; broader scope EDITORIAL. |
| Excitement | A stirred-up sense of engaging possibility or activity. | Present or anticipated event, novelty, or challenge. | Something feels involving or energizing. This label does not establish a physiological measurement. | LAZZARO §1a; DESMET Activity-focus example. |
| Fear | Apprehension about possible harm or an unwanted outcome. | Threatening event, consequence, or entity. | The person perceives danger or uncertain escape. Fear need not concern only immediate or certain threats. | OCC-REVIEW §2; LAZZARO Fear row; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Fear. |
| Anxiety | Apprehensive unease amid uncertainty about a consequential situation. | Uncertain prospects, possible evaluation, or unclear threat. | The person worries about what could happen. This is a design-language sense, not a diagnosis or sharp universal fear/anxiety boundary. | EDITORIAL; OCC-REVIEW groups anxious tokens under fear rather than establishing this split. |
| Dread | Heavy apprehension about something unwelcome that feels ahead. | Anticipated adverse event or encounter. | Waiting for a confrontation, reveal, or loss. Unlike surprise, the anticipated event need not be unexpected. | EDITORIAL; fear-family vocabulary in OCC-REVIEW §2. |
| Suspense | Tense involvement while an important outcome is unresolved. | Pending resolution and its consequences. | A rescue, contest, or revelation could turn out differently. Can combine hope, fear, curiosity, and excitement. | EDITORIAL; a temporal experience, not a demonstrated independent emotion category. |
| Despair | A felt collapse of hope about something important. | Valued future or possibility of improvement. | A situation seems to offer no acceptable way forward. Do not infer actual impossibility from the feeling. | EDITORIAL. |

## Attention, discovery, and sensory experience

| Term | Working definition | Target | Possible elicitor/appraisal | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interest | Engaged attention toward something worth considering further. | Topic, activity, situation, or object. | Further attention seems worthwhile. Interest is not proof of learning, pleasure, or absorption. | SMITH-ELLSWORTH, Interest and hope; DESMET discussion. |
| Curiosity | Wanting to find out more about a question or possibility. | Knowledge gap, unfamiliar relation, or unexplored option. | An unanswered possibility invites investigation. Knowing how to investigate can coexist with curiosity. | LAZZARO Easy Fun/§3c; DESMET Object-focus. |
| Fascination | Strong attraction to examining or understanding something compelling. | Object, pattern, subject, or phenomenon. | Its qualities hold attention and invite closer engagement. No numeric boundary separates it from interest. | DESMET Figure 1 and General Discussion. |
| Wonder | Amazed attention to something strikingly unusual or extraordinary. | Remarkable occurrence, object, or possibility. | What is encountered exceeds ordinary expectation while inviting contemplation. | LAZZARO §3a; working wording. |
| Awe | Overwhelmed wonder at something experienced as imposing or extraordinary. | Phenomenon, scale, power, or significance. | The encounter can feel magnificent, daunting, or both. Fear is possible, not mandatory in this broader working sense. | EDITORIAL extension; LAZZARO §3a uses a narrower wonder-with-dread account. |
| Surprise | Being taken aback by a departure from expectation. | Unexpected occurrence or information. | A rule behaves differently, an outcome changes, or something is revealed. Valence can change with interpretation. | LAZZARO Surprise row; DESMET Figure 1 foregrounds pleasant surprise; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Surprise. |
| Confusion | Not having a coherent account of what is happening or what something means. | Current situation, information, rule, or relation. | Cues conflict or an explanation fails. It can accompany curiosity, fear, amusement, or disengagement. | EDITORIAL; not established from mere source-label membership. |
| Boredom | Unpleasant disengagement from something offering little worth attending to. | Present activity or situation. | Play feels tedious or uninteresting. Repetition can instead be soothing; stillness is not evidence of boredom. | LAZZARO §2a; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Boredom. |
| Amusement | Pleasure in something read as funny or playfully entertaining. | Comic event, remark, interaction, or incongruity. | An unexpected social exchange or playful mishap is interpreted humorously. Laughter alone does not prove amusement. | LAZZARO §4a; DESMET Figure 1. |
| Enchantment | Being captivated by something felt as delightful or extraordinary. | Experience, presentation, object, or imagined world. | A scene, sound, movement, or encounter draws the person in. Not evidence of total absorption or loss of control. | DESMET Figure 1. |
| Inspiration | A felt surge of creative possibility or impulse. | Possible creation, action, or direction. | Encountering something makes a new course seem worth pursuing. It does not mean that course is adopted. | DESMET Figure 1 and General Discussion. |
| Aesthetic pleasure | Pleasure in perceiving or appreciating sensory or compositional qualities. | Sound, form, motion, texture, rhythm, or composition. | The encounter is felt as beautiful, graceful, evocative, or otherwise rewarding. Not equivalent to MDA aesthetics as a whole. | EDITORIAL; DESMET Object-focus supports sensory sources, not this as a separate validated category. |

## Relationships and other people's fortunes

| Term | Working definition | Target | Possible elicitor/appraisal | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Love / affection | Felt warmth, attachment, or care toward someone or something valued. | Person, creature, meaningful object, or relationship. | Presence, memory, or interaction brings the attachment into experience. Love can also name an enduring relationship/attitude. | DESMET Figure 1 and Meaning-focus; LAZZARO §4d; source senses vary. |
| Compassion / sympathy | Concern for another's suffering, with a wish that it be eased. | Suffering person and their situation. | Their distress matters to the experiencer. DESMET clusters the words; other supplied meanings may distinguish them. | DESMET Figure 1 and Stage 2 appraisal discussion. |
| Pity | Sorrowful concern about another's misfortune. | Other person and adverse outcome. | Their situation is understood as bad for them. This model sense does not establish condescension or active help. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1. |
| Happy-for / shared gladness | Gladness at another person's good fortune. | Other person and their welcome outcome. | Their success or benefit is welcomed without needing a mentoring relationship or personal credit. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1. |
| Naches | Warm pleasure or pride in a child or mentee's accomplishment. | That person and their achievement. | Someone in a nurturing/mentoring relationship succeeds. Distinguish pride in their progress from pride in one's own teaching. | LAZZARO Emotions During Play and §4d; relational use in this report. |
| Gratitude | Appreciative pleasure concerning another's welcome contribution. | Contributor, action, and beneficial consequence. | Help or care is valued. Admiration without a welcome contribution can be a different reading; repayment is not required here. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1. |
| Admiration | Appreciative regard for another person's action, ability, or achievement. | Other person and the quality admired. | Skill, creativity, or conduct merits recognition, even without benefit to oneself. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1; DESMET Figure 1 and Other-focus. |
| Respect | Regard for someone as worthy or valuable. | Person, conduct, or valued quality. | How they act or what they represent merits regard. Respect may also be an attitude or enacted treatment. | DESMET Figure 1; episode/attitude distinction retained. |
| Reverent devotion / worship | Exalted, devoted regard toward someone held in high esteem. | Revered person in the inspected source sense. | The person feels worthy of honor and devotion. Religious, object-directed, or other meanings need their own context. | DESMET Figure 1 and Typology of Positive Emotions. |
| Yearning | A felt pull toward an absent or not-yet-attained person, place, or experience. | Wanted connection or experience. | Distance or absence makes the wanted thing salient. It may be sweet, painful, or mixed. | EDITORIAL. |
| Loneliness | Painful felt lack of wanted connection. | Own social connection or a particular absent relationship. | Available contact does not feel sufficient or meaningful. Being alone need not mean loneliness. | EDITORIAL; no age, player-type, or clinical inference. |
| Nostalgia | Affectionate, potentially bittersweet feeling about something from the past. | Remembered time, place, relationship, or activity. | A sound, object, or event evokes valued memories. Familiarity alone does not establish nostalgia. | NOBA, other emotions/context discussion; original working wording. |
| Envy | Uncomfortable wanting in relation to another's advantage or possession. | Other's advantage and one's own comparison or lack. | Their position makes a desired difference salient. Distinguish wanting their advantage from enjoying their misfortune. | EDITORIAL; not silently substituted for OCC-REVIEW resentment. |
| Jealousy | Unease about losing or sharing a valued relationship or position. | Valued connection and a perceived competitor/threat. | Attention or loyalty seems at risk. This working distinction from envy is not asserted universal across languages or uses. | EDITORIAL. |
| Schadenfreude | Pleasure in another person's misfortune. | Other's setback, often a rival's in the game report. | Their loss is welcome to the experiencer. It is not automatically earned personal triumph or a feature to encourage. | LAZZARO Emotions During Play; OCC-REVIEW gloating comparison. |

| Term | Working definition | Target | Possible elicitor/appraisal | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anger | Oppositional displeasure toward an obstruction, offense, or blamed harm. | Obstructed goal, wrongful action, or responsible person. | A setback or conduct is interpreted as thwarting or blameworthy. Some source accounts emphasize different combinations. | LAZZARO §2a; OCC-REVIEW Table 1; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Anger. |
| Frustration | Strained displeasure at being unable to make wanted progress. | Blocked activity, goal, or failed attempt. | Effort does not produce expected progress. Repeated failure is one possibility, not a required cause or a good design recipe. | LAZZARO §2a; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Frustration. |
| Resentment | Lingering grievance about perceived unfairness, treatment, or an unwelcome advantage. | Treatment, relationship, or distribution as interpreted. | The person feels wronged or passed over. The narrower OCC sense concerns displeasure at another's good fortune. | EDITORIAL broad sense; OCC-REVIEW Table 1 only supports its specified narrower sense. |
| Reproach | Blaming disapproval of another person's conduct. | Another's action. | Their conduct is judged blameworthy. A spoken reproach is an expression, not necessarily a distinct feeling. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1. |
| Guilt | Self-blaming unease about perceived wrongful conduct or an unmet obligation. | Own action, omission, and responsibility. | The person believes they did wrong. It need not involve public exposure or an actual rule violation established by the reviewer. | SMITH-ELLSWORTH Shame and guilt. |
| Shame | Painful self-evaluation in relation to a perceived failing. | Self in relation to conduct, exposure, or a standard. | The person feels they fall short. | Remorse | Painful self-reproach concerning one's wrong action and its harmful consequences. | Own conduct together with resulting harm. | Responsibility for a bad result is experienced as blameworthy. Broader regret need not imply moral fault. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1; not an exhaustive account of everyday use. |
| Embarrassment | Self-conscious discomfort about how one appears in a situation. | Own presentation or a social moment. | A blunder or exposure makes evaluation salient. It need not entail deep moral wrongdoing. | EDITORIAL; NOBA discusses embarrassment after a social faux pas, not a full definition. |
| Contempt | Disdainful devaluation of another's conduct or character. | Person or conduct judged beneath regard. | A perceived violation invites dismissal rather than only opposition. Anger and contempt can overlap. | EDITORIAL gloss; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Anger and contempt found similar profiles, not a clean universal boundary. |
| Disgust | Repelled rejection of something experienced as revolting. | Material, behavior, situation, or expressed attitude. | Something is read as physically or evaluatively repulsive. Mere dislike or unfamiliarity does not establish disgust. | LAZZARO Disgust row; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Disgust. |
| Grief | Sorrow and related feelings around the loss of someone or something deeply valued. | Lost relationship, person, place, possibility, or way of life. | Loss gives absence significance. Grief may be a process with many feelings, not one brief state. | EDITORIAL; SMITH-ELLSWORTH Sadness includes bereavement examples, not this full definition. |
| Sadness | Sorrow about an unwanted situation, loss, or disappointment. | Valued thing lost or adverse circumstances. | Something matters and is not as wanted. Source descriptions of low control are contextual, not a claim that sadness requires helplessness. | SMITH-ELLSWORTH Sadness; DESMET Stage 2 loss example. |
| Distress | Being troubled or upset by an unwanted development. | Adverse consequence or current situation. | What happens matters and reads as undesirable. This is a broad feeling word, not a clinical diagnosis. | OCC-REVIEW Table 1. |

## Rest, intensity, and inner state

These words may describe an episode, a mood, or another aspect of felt experience. State the intended sense rather than reject them as illegitimate emotions.

| Term | Working definition | Target | Possible elicitor/appraisal | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contentment | Settled pleasure in how things currently stand. | Present circumstances or recent experiences. | The moment seems worth savoring without needing another achievement. No hard boundary from satisfaction is claimed. | DESMET General Discussion; experience gloss rather than a separate Figure 1 type. |
| Calm | Felt steadiness or low agitation. | Own current condition; sometimes no clear object. | A situation allows or accompanies composure. Calm is not guaranteed by slower action or easy goals. | EDITORIAL; no physiological or therapeutic claim. |
| Relaxation | Felt easing of tension or concern. | Own condition or relaxing activity. | Strain subsides or the person feels less constrained. Can coexist with interest; may be a mood rather than a brief emotion. | DESMET Figure 1 and Activity-focus; LAZZARO §1a. |
| Euphoria | An intense, buoyant sense of joy. | Welcome experience or diffuse current condition. | A moment feels exceptionally uplifting. No brain-chemical explanation or clinical inference follows. | DESMET Figure 1. |
| Feeling energized | A lively sense of activation or vitality. | Own state in relation to an activity or encounter. | Interaction feels enlivening. It may denote a mood or bodily state rather than a discrete emotion. | DESMET Figure 1 and discussion of mood/trait usage. |
| Dreaminess | Quiet, reflective absorption in thoughts or imagination. | Inner train of thought, imagined possibilities, or diffuse state. | Attention turns inward. It need not be relaxing or pleasant in every supplied use. | DESMET Figure 1 and mood discussion; reflective sense. |

## Useful distinctions and transitions

- **Completion, satisfaction, relief, fiero:** the same win can fulfill a want, remove an unpleasant burden, or feel personally hard-won. It can do several, or none. A victory animation does not decide the interpretation.
- **Personal pride, naches, admiration, gratitude:** distinguish favorable self-evaluation, pleasure in a learner's progress, regard for another's skill/conduct, and appreciation of a welcome contribution. They can coexist in one relationship.
- **Disappointment, regret, guilt, remorse:** a wanted outcome can fail without a bad personal choice; wishing something otherwise need not involve moral fault; self-blame and concern about caused harm can add other meanings. These are working distinctions, not disjoint psychological bins.
- **Envy, jealousy, resentment, schadenfreude:** another's advantage, a threatened valued connection, perceived unfairness, and another's misfortune are different possible targets. Do not diagnose any from competitive behavior alone.
- **Curiosity, confusion, wonder, suspense:** wanting to know more differs from lacking a coherent account, contemplating the extraordinary, or waiting for an important resolution. One person can experience combinations; another may disengage.
- **Fear, anxiety, dread:** names can emphasize threat, unsettled apprehension, or anticipated unwantedness. Their boundaries depend on usage and source. Never infer a disorder or force future/present into an absolute dividing rule.
- **Love, nostalgia, sadness, relief:** memory, relationship, and current circumstance can produce mixed experiences. Feeling pleasant is not the same as being beneficial; feeling unpleasant is not automatically a design defect.

For a transition, identify whether the experiencer stays the same, what the feeling concerns before/after, and what changes in the person's interpretation. Preserve overlap, reversals, ambiguity, and a requested short interval. Consult only useful vocabulary; no sequence must pass through these groups.

## Extend the vocabulary

An unlisted term is welcome. Capture its supplied meaning, experiencer, relevant target, a distinguishing example, and provenance where useful. Ask only when competing meanings materially change the current work. Different names may share a meaning; the same name may have different meanings.

Use sources to clarify a particular published usage, not to overrule a user's explicitly different intention. Label original working conventions and proposed game adaptations. Do not invent an etymology, universal cultural meaning, origin story, necessary elicitor, prevalence statistic, or neural mechanism to lend authority to a word. Do not automatically add glossary records or make a new schema.

## Sources and limits

[Source index and fidelity notes](README.md) distinguish original meaning support, source-context limits, editorial working conventions, and user-supplied usage. The [research orientation](research/first-blush/answer-analyst.md) and [discovery journal](research/discovery-journal.md) retain first-pass leads and failures; they are not an exhaustive literature review or independent evidence for every row.

Newly inspected Desmet Figure 1 supports several entries beyond the first-pass analyst's text-only reading. The working collection is not Desmet's typology, OCC, Four Keys, GEW, or a union claimed to be scientifically validated. No official rating instrument, full translated dictionary, intensity scoring, or fixed emotion inventory is implemented. Human review remains appropriate, especially for broad editorial senses and transfer to a specific audience, culture, or game.
