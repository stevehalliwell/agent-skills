## What changed and why

This is the principal version source for the framework guide's six paired groups. It reports refinement after more than 30,000 people completed the profile. New test items were interspersed with existing measures, allowing authors to investigate candidate dimensions while updating existing scales.

Two changes matter when tracing terminology:

1. Discovery became a distinct dimension, paired with Design, formerly called Customization. Their new parent group was Creativity.
2. Earlier Strategy group became Mastery; its dimensions became Challenge and Strategy. Author connects renaming to feedback that a combined score confused preference for strategic planning with preference for difficult gameplay.

That distinction matters: someone can want thoughtful decisions without wanting punishing execution or extreme difficulty. A broad group score can obscure those differences.

## Reported empirical procedure

Article lists sizeable English-speaking respondent groups, including US/Canada (6,222), Indonesia (6,000), EU (2,004), and other regions. These counts describe contributors to this development stage, not population shares or a present-day audience census.

Authors ran factor analysis separately for US/Canada, Indonesia, and EU, then across the full dataset. They report the same number and composition of factors in those analyses. This concerns **structure**, not equal average preferences between regions. Shared dimensions can coexist with different score distributions.

After identifying 12 factors, authors used hierarchical clustering to describe their relationships. Factor identification and clustering play different roles: one identifies dimensions in item responses; the other organizes similarities between those dimensions. The six pairs should not be mistaken for six independent or exclusive player types.

## Vocabulary map

Published group and dimension names below are retained for exact reference. Descriptions are independent shorthand, not replacement scale definitions.

- **Action — Destruction / Excitement:** spectacular disruption and intense tempo are related, but not identical, interests.
- **Social — Competition / Community:** other players can matter as opponents, collaborators, or social company.
- **Mastery — Challenge / Strategy:** testing and improving skill differs from enjoying deliberation and planning.
- **Achievement — Completion / Power:** finishing available goals differs from increasing capability or status inside a game.
- **Creativity — Design / Discovery:** making expressive choices differs from probing possibilities and experimenting with systems.
- **Immersion — Fantasy / Story:** inhabiting another identity or setting differs from investment in narrative and characters.

The article's Immersion introduction says “3 underlying motivations” but lists only Fantasy and Story. Its explicit 12-factor claim, six-pair explanation, and description of Creativity clarify the intended two-item grouping. Preserve that discrepancy as a source erratum, not a thirteenth factor.

## Higher-level organization

Article describes three broader branches:

- Immersion–Exploration: engagement with a world's narrative, identity, design, and possibilities. “Exploration” is the branch wording used in this section; it is not a replacement for the six-pair label Creativity.
- Achievement–Mastery: progress, accomplishment, capability, and practiced competence.
- Action–Social: energetic experiences, social engagement, and stimulation.

Yee compares the last branch with the broader psychological meaning of Extraversion. That is the author's explanatory analogy. It does not authorize deriving personality traits from a player's actions or from an unadministered profile.

## What this source establishes—and does not

Establishes author-reported development history, exact v2 labels, rationale for revisions, and reported similarity of factor structure in selected regional samples.

Does not provide, within these notes:

- Full inventory, scoring weights, norms, raw data, or reproducible statistical analysis.
- Independent psychometric replication or proof of universal cross-cultural invariance.
- A representative census of gamers: participation and English-language sampling remain limits.
- Causal evidence that a mechanic produces a motivation, enjoyment, retention, or commercial success.
- Evidence about this repository's example game's actual players.

Authors say weights and norms were revised for v2. Reusing labels alone does not reproduce a valid measurement tool. An observed behavior can have several explanations; retries, for example, do not uniquely establish Challenge interest.
