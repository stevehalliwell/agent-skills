## Findings

### Learning account

Koster treats games as simplified systems people can understand through play. Their rules expose relationships without reproducing every detail of real situations. His explanation of enjoyment centers on acquiring useful internal representations and practicing them until they become readily usable.

The argument distinguishes recognizing a relationship from being able to use it reliably. Practice therefore has a role within his learning account; the presence of repetition alone does not tell a researcher whether discovery has ended.

### Continuing challenge

The essay predicts declining interest when players have exhausted a game’s underlying intellectual challenge. Koster proposes interacting players as one way a game can continue presenting difficult-to-predict situations. He also criticizes designs that permit only a single prescribed resolution and argues for systems capable of sustaining interpretation.

These are claims about available relationships and possibilities, not a claim that changing graphics or increasing numerical difficulty necessarily creates new learning.

### Wider purpose

The article’s main public argument concerns games’ cultural and educational legitimacy. Koster connects the complexity of a work’s interpretation to its capacity to challenge an audience’s assumptions. The learning theory is part of an argument about games as a medium, not merely a formula for maximizing retention.

## Evidence boundaries

- Primary evidence for what Koster argued in 2005; not an experimental test of learning causing enjoyment.
- Claims about cognition, education, and artistic maturity belong to an opinion essay. The article does not supply a controlled study, measurement protocol, or validated fun score.
- Its strong prediction about eventual boredom should not be silently softened into an empirically established law, nor extended to every form of pleasure. Read alongside [author’s 2008 clarification](koster-2008-fun-in-class-source-notes.md).
- No observations of the workbench’s example game or its players appear in this source. Transfer to that game would require separate evidence.
