## Main contribution

The paper separates **amount of motivation** from **kind of motivation**. Two people can work equally hard while acting for very different reasons: personal interest, an endorsed goal, pressure, approval, or reward. Similar observable persistence therefore need not mean similar experience or well-being. See printed pp. 68–70.

SDT treats social conditions as contributors to motivation, rather than treating engagement entirely as a fixed trait of the person. The three needs organize hypotheses about those conditions:

- **Autonomy:** acting with personal endorsement. This is not equivalent to independence, selfishness, or acting without other people. A dependent or collective action can be autonomous. The explicit clarification appears on p. 74.
- **Competence:** experiencing effectiveness. In the account of intrinsic motivation, effective action alone is insufficient if the action is experienced as controlled. Feedback and challenge must be interpreted alongside autonomy. See p. 70.
- **Relatedness:** connection and belonging. This matters to motivation and internalization, but the paper also explicitly recognizes enjoyable intrinsically motivated activity performed alone. Immediate social interaction is not a universal prerequisite for interest. See pp. 71, 73.

The paper proposes these needs as broadly important to healthy functioning. It does not establish that each short activity must contain a visible feature for each need, or that a designer can diagnose a person from a feature list.

## Intrinsic interest, rewards, and context

Cognitive evaluation theory examines conditions that sustain or undermine interest in doing an activity for itself. The reviewed work links manageable challenge and useful feedback with competence, and links choice and noncontrolling social treatment with autonomy. See pp. 70–71.

The reward discussion concerns how events change the experienced reason for acting. The authors report evidence that expected tangible rewards contingent on performance can diminish intrinsic motivation. Do not compress this into “all rewards are bad” or assume that every in-game feedback event is equivalent to the reward manipulations reviewed here. A claim about a particular game economy requires evidence about what players experience and how that economy operates.

The authors explicitly limit this account to activities that already offer some interest, novelty, challenge, or aesthetic appeal. Supporting needs is not presented as a device that automatically makes any otherwise uninteresting task intrinsically enjoyable. See p. 71.

## Extrinsic motivation is not a single category

Organismic integration theory distinguishes reasons for pursuing an outcome beyond the activity itself. These distinctions explain why “intrinsic versus extrinsic” is too coarse. See pp. 71–74.

- **External regulation:** action depends on an outside demand or contingency.
- **Introjected regulation:** pressure has moved inside the person without becoming an endorsed commitment; guilt and conditional self-worth can sustain effort.
- **Identified regulation:** the person accepts the goal as personally important.
- **Integrated regulation:** that accepted goal fits coherently with the person's other values and commitments.

Identified and integrated regulation can be autonomous while remaining extrinsic. Integration is not the same as doing something because it is inherently enjoyable. The continuum is not a mandatory sequence through which everyone must pass for every behavior. See pp. 72–73.

The review connects internalization with social connection, sufficient competence, meaningful explanation, and support for volition. Its discussion of rationale for uninteresting behavior provides a basis for investigating how people understand requirements, not for asserting that explanatory text alone produces autonomy. See pp. 73–74.

## What the evidence can and cannot support

The article surveys experimental, field, cross-cultural, and within-person work. Its discussion includes differences between people's average experiences and fluctuations across roles or days. Need-related experience is therefore contextual, not simply a permanent player category. See pp. 75–76.

General theory and findings from education, health care, work, and relationships do not directly validate a specific game mechanic. The review's broad well-being claims must not be converted into clinical judgments about a player or promises that a game improves mental health.

For game-specific evidence, read [Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski's four-study paper](ryan-rigby-przybylski-2006-video-games-notes.md). Neither source supplies evidence about this workbench's example game.
