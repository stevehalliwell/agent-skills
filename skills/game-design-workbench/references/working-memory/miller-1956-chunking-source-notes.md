## Research substance

### Different tasks imply different limits

Miller examines absolute judgment of stimulus magnitudes, immediate recall, and rapid apprehension of small sets. Similar-looking numerical results do not establish one common mechanism. His absolute-judgment examples concern identifying an isolated stimulus along a dimension such as pitch or loudness, not remembering a whole interface. Adding dimensions changes the identification problem and can substantially increase distinguishable alternatives. [Printed pp. 83–90.]

In immediate-memory studies, increasing information carried by each item does not shrink recall span enough to preserve a constant total information quantity. Miller therefore distinguishes information measured in bits from familiar units used in recall. This distinction is central to the paper; the title alone misses it. [“The Span of Immediate Memory,” pp. 90–93.]

### Learned organization changes the effective unit

What counts as one recalled unit depends on prior learning. A familiar word can operate as a unit even though it contains several speech sounds. The same physical input need not have the same psychological grouping for different people. Miller explicitly treats the boundaries of a chunk as a problem requiring explanation, not as obvious item boundaries. [P. 93.]

His telegraph example illustrates progressively larger learned units: isolated signals become letters, then words and phrases. Increased recall of the original signals can follow improved organization rather than a larger basic storage capacity. [“Recoding,” p. 93.]

### Recoding has a learning cost

Miller describes Sidney Smith's binary-digit recoding demonstration. Participants learned codes that replaced groups of binary digits with new symbols. Brief instruction improved recall, but larger recoding ratios initially fell short of predictions based on already familiar digits. Translation had not become fluent enough to keep up with incoming material. More extensive self-practice produced much larger gains. This demonstration supports a role for practiced recoding, not a guarantee that any newly labeled group instantly becomes one chunk. [Pp. 93–95.]

### Organization can also distort retrieval

Recoding is not merely lossless storage compression. Verbal descriptions can shape later reconstruction of an event. Miller connects this to evidence that names influence reproduction of visual figures and to distortions in eyewitness testimony. The remembered interpretation can substitute for details of the original event. [P. 95.]

## Evidence boundaries

- Historical synthesis, not contemporary consensus statement or game-play study.
- Smith's extended-practice result includes self-experimentation; do not treat it as population-level training evidence.
- Miller warns against equating immediate-memory span with absolute-judgment span. He ends without endorsing one deep explanation for every appearance of seven. [Pp. 91–92, 96.]
- Source does not establish a maximum number of menu entries, mechanics, rules, skill atoms, or simultaneous visible objects.
- Source does not establish whether a persistent cue improves any particular game. That needs a concrete task, presentation, and observations.
