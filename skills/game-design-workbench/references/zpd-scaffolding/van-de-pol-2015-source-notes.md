## Study design

Thirty social studies teachers and 768 students aged 12–15 participated in a five-lesson European Union project in pre-vocational education. Seventeen teachers received a scaffolding intervention; 13 did not. Schools were assigned alternately by confirmation order, with one condition per school to reduce contamination. This is not equivalent to unconstrained individual random assignment.

The intervention combined theoretical instruction, video observation and teacher reflection. Researchers measured support contingency and independent group working time, alongside achievement, task effort and appreciation of support. Contingency concerns how control changes in response to students' understanding, not how much control a teacher uses in isolation.

## Findings worth preserving together

1. The intervention increased contingency. Independent working time also increased, so support quality and time between teacher visits changed together in the classroom context.
2. Achievement improved over time, but the predicted occasion-by-contingency interaction was not significant for either the multiple-choice test or knowledge assignment.
3. A three-way interaction involving occasion, contingency and independent working time became significant for the multiple-choice test **when task effort was included as a covariate**. With shorter independent periods, lower contingency was associated with better gains; with longer periods, higher contingency was associated with better gains. The knowledge-assignment analysis did not show the corresponding significant interaction, including after adjustment.
4. Task-effort patterns also depended on independent working time. Longer independent periods were associated with declining task effort at both contingency levels, with a smaller decline under higher contingency.
5. Higher contingency related to greater appreciation of support. Appreciation is a distinct outcome, not a substitute for achievement or independent transfer.

## Limits and interpretation

The study does not justify a universal “more contingent help always works better” rule. Effects depend on outcome, statistical adjustment and the spacing of help. Intervention duration was relatively short, about 8 weeks. The authors call for further work on mechanisms and contextual factors.

These are classroom findings about small groups and human teachers, not experiments on game hints or authored level routes. Longer independent working time is not itself evidence of mastery; students can disengage while working alone. The paper supplies evidence for context-sensitive hypotheses, not proof of this example game's player experience.
