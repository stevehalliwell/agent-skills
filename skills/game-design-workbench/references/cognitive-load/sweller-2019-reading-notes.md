## Main findings and qualifications

1. **Prior knowledge changes what constitutes an element.** In “Human Cognitive Architecture Used in 1998” and “Categories of Cognitive Load,” authors distinguish processing unfamiliar information from using organized knowledge already stored in long-term memory. What requires several interdependent operations for a novice may function as one learned unit for an expert. Counting visible items without considering knowledge does not establish cognitive demand.

2. **Element interactivity concerns relationships that must be processed together.** “Categories of Cognitive Load” treats complexity as an interaction between material and learner knowledge. Both task content and presentation procedures can generate interacting elements. A long inventory of operations does not, by itself, identify the relationships active at one moment.

3. **Problem search and knowledge construction need not coincide.** “Goal-Free Effect” describes how means–ends search can require simultaneous management of current state, target state, their differences, available operations, and subgoals. Reducing goal specificity changes that search process in instructional tasks. This is not a general prescription to remove goals from games; the reviewed outcome is learning, not enjoyment or challenge quality.

4. **Examples can support learning, but effects have boundaries.** “Worked Example Effect” discusses learning from supplied solution steps rather than generating every step. “Completion Problem Effect” describes partial solutions that learners must finish. “Expertise Reversal Effect” and “Guidance-Fading Effect” explain why the same help can become redundant or counterproductive as knowledge grows. An example should not be assumed useful merely because it exists; learners can skim it without processing relevant relationships.

5. **Split attention and redundancy are different problems.** In “Split-Attention Effect,” separate sources each lack information needed to understand the whole, so learners must integrate them mentally. In “Redundancy Effect,” sources can each stand alone, and processing duplicated information can add unnecessary work. Integration and deletion are therefore not interchangeable recommendations.

6. **Persistence and pacing can matter independently of content.** “Transient Information Effect” describes information that vanishes before later processing is complete. Retention demands can undermine advantages of spoken or animated presentation. Self-pacing and segmentation sometimes help; prior knowledge and duration constrain these effects. The paper does not support a universal preference for either animation or static display.

7. **Less load is not always better learning.** “Variability Effect” discusses situations where varied practice increases demand while improving transfer, provided learners can handle it. “Categories of Cognitive Load” also revises older terminology: germane processing is treated as allocation of resources toward intrinsic task information, not a third independent load to add to an intrinsic-plus-extraneous total.

8. **Measurement requires more than observing difficulty.** “Measuring Cognitive Load” distinguishes subjective effort ratings, multi-item questionnaires, secondary tasks, and physiological techniques. Overall effort ratings do not cleanly separate intrinsic from extraneous load. Rating timing and wording can change estimates; secondary tasks can disrupt the task being studied; physiological measures need validation. These findings do not supply a calibrated score for game mechanics or a diagnosis from failure duration.

9. **Instructional purpose affects whether a demand is incidental.** “Emotions, Stress and Uncertainty” distinguishes unwanted interference from demands that are part of the skill being learned. The authors frame several extensions as future research rather than settled interventions. Difficulty cannot be classified as unnecessary without stating the learning purpose.

## Evidence limits

- Primary author account of cognitive load theory; underlying experiment claims are synthesized by authors. Consult individual studies before relying on precise populations, sample sizes, effect sizes, or experimental procedures.
- Educational learning and transfer are central outcomes. This source does not validate the example game's player experience, establish universal mechanic limits, or prove which intended game demands should be preserved.
- The 1988 foundational paper is cited and discussed by this review. It was not read in full when these notes were written.
