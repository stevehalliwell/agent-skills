# Emotional experience — intent, meaning, and transitions

Use when authoring/refining an `aesthetics` intention or when the user wants to name, develop, or inspect a felt experience or emotional sequence. Aesthetics is a primary route into the [vocabulary](../references/emotional-experience/emotions.md), not a requirement to select another framework or begin a separate emotion interview. This is shared workbench guidance, not a nineteenth framework, required intake form, or emotion-detection instrument. It supports plain language as well as precise cultural or user-defined terms.

## Match the depth to the request

Support the range from a simple direct game to an emotionally intricate one. A clear mechanic, audience, or plain experiential goal is valid without an elaborate emotion specification. Capture supplied intent; do not require the designer to browse a vocabulary, name an emotion, or complete every prompt before useful work begins.

For example, “Flappy Bird, but for older players” supplies a reference and audience direction, not reaction-time limits, visual acuity, input capacity, preferences, or a medical profile. Recover what should be kept or changed when that affects the design. Ask about relevant people and play conditions rather than deriving capabilities from age. Difficulty, learning support, input, legibility, and session shape remain questions until supplied or investigated. Do not reinterpret this brief as a mandatory goal of relaxation, simplicity, nostalgia, or therapeutic benefit.

For an emotion-led brief, make the desired experience precise enough to discuss. “Quiet satisfaction” can be more useful than a technical term. A dictionary label is optional; the user's account of the experience matters. Dictionary recognition, publication, and etymology are not validity gates. Established, cultural, personal, and coined terms can all express precise design intent; provenance identifies the working meaning, not a hierarchy of legitimate feelings. In game-explorer mode, a possible emotional direction remains a proposal, not an adopted goal.

The [vocabulary](../references/emotional-experience/emotions.md) offers a broad but non-exhaustive set of working distinctions. Consult relevant entries only. It does not delimit possible experiences, rank feelings, diagnose people, or require coverage of its groups.

## Distinguish feeling, target, and elicitor

Use these as thinking aids, not required record fields:

- **Experiencer:** whose feeling is under discussion? Player, helper, rival, spectator, character, and designer are different people/roles. A character's performed emotion is not evidence the player shares it.
- **Feeling:** what quality of experience is meant? Preserve the user's words, intensity, ambiguity, and cultural or personal meaning. Separate a feeling from a gesture, motive, need, focused state, or judgment about design quality.
- **Target:** who or what the feeling concerns. Personal accomplishment, a learner's achievement, another person's assistance, a threatened relationship, and an imagined outcome can give similar-looking reactions different meanings. Some moods have no clear object; do not invent one.
- **Elicitor/appraisal:** the situation and its meaning to this person that could contribute to the feeling. Distinguish a success animation from interpreting success as personally earned. “Source” here can mean an eliciting situation; bibliographic sources for terms are separate and linked in the vocabulary.
- **Conditions:** prior knowledge, investment, expectations, relationships, perceived control, context, and what the person believes happened. Unknown conditions stay unknown.
- **Evidence status:** authored intention, hypothetical sequence, source-reported account, observation, participant interpretation, or supported conclusion. Do not promote one into another.

A vocabulary entry's target and eliciting situations are aids to interpretation, not exclusive definitions or necessary/sufficient conditions. A single event can support several meanings or none. Do not translate these notes into a formula mapping mechanics to emotion.

## Develop a selected emotional moment

1. **Recover the actual intent.** State the bounded activity, whose experience matters, and whether discussing a target, exploration lead, or observed event. Done: existing intent and new interpretations are distinct.
2. **Clarify only consequential meaning.** Use the user's description or a relevant vocabulary entry. For unfamiliar/coined/culturally specific words, preserve spelling and ask what experience is meant when a different reading would change design. If the intended meaning is not supplied, label it unresolved rather than inventing a definition, guessing a translation, or treating the word as invalid. Done: the working meaning or its limits are explicit.
3. **Locate the emotional target.** Identify who/what matters and why the event could matter to this person. Done: personal pride, pride in a learner, admiration of skill, and gratitude for help are not conflated merely because someone celebrates.
4. **Trace a possible route.** Connect cues, available actions, consequences, and the person's interpretation to a possible feeling. Label imagined rules or reactions. Done: an actual rule or contextual assumption supports each step, or the step is explicitly hypothetical.
5. **Construct an alternative reading.** Ask how the same event could produce relief, resentment, indifference, or another plausible experience. Include possible social costs and mismatched interpretations. Done: a counterexample can challenge the preferred account without blaming a player.
6. **Return the useful detail.** Give the intended or candidate feeling, meaningful target, route, transition if relevant, unknowns, and evidence question. Include only useful parts. Done: designer can inspect the interpretation without adopting a rule, commissioning a study, or filling a new schema.

Do not equate a negative feeling with a design defect or an intense feeling with success. Context, consent, duration, purpose, recovery, and the person's own appraisal matter. Strong discomfort, guilt, humiliation, fear, or social pressure are not neutral engagement tools; surface foreseeable costs rather than recommend coercion as a route to retention.

## Trace transitions and mixtures

A target can concern a sequence, an overlap, or an abrupt reversal rather than a single label. Preserve the requested relationship between feelings.

An optional prose trace:

> Person and context; event as understood; possible feeling toward an object/person; changed information or action; next interpretation/feeling; aftermath.

This trace expresses an interpretation, not deterministic causation. Describe links in words when arrows would hide uncertainty.

For a proposed transition, inspect:

- Whether it is the **same experiencer** or a shift between people. A learner's triumph and a mentor's naches may coexist; neither proves a transition within one person.
- Whether the **target or appraisal changes**. New information about responsibility, help, risk, or another person's outcome may change a feeling without changing visible game state.
- Whether feelings **replace, overlap, oscillate, or remain ambiguous**. Do not force a single-label timeline.
- The **cue and opportunity** that could make the change intelligible. A sudden stimulus is not automatically a meaningful emotional reversal.
- The intended **interval, intensity, and aftermath**. “Only a breath between them” expresses a tight experiential interval unless the user actually defines a physiological timing requirement. Do not invent seconds, thresholds, or guaranteed response times.
- The strongest **counterreading**. The intended reversal might instead feel confusing, manipulative, flat, or emotionally continuous.

Do not require all scenes to follow a universal arc, frustration before triumph, or alternating high/low intensity. Pacing curves are hypotheses, not emotion measurements.

## Simple direct game example

**Supplied brief:** “Flappy Bird, but for older players.” No emotional objective or specific accommodation supplied.

**Faithful capture:** a simple reference-led game direction with an intended audience. The reference does not adopt its exact difficulty, punishment, controls, or business model. No assumed deficit, ability score, or personality profile follows from the audience label.

**Useful bounded work:** clarify which part of the reference matters for the requested change; inspect the relevant action, feedback, readability, retry, or play setting. Keep other choices open. If the user later asks for a calm, playful, competitive, or demanding experience, capture that direction then.

**Avoid:** a compulsory interview across sixty emotion terms; prescribing low difficulty because of age; claiming slower movement creates calm; inventing a desire for Flow, fiero, or therapy. This workflow need not be loaded further once the brief is usable for the requested operation.

## Specific emotional arc example

**Illustrative target:** pride in someone the player helped, followed closely by regret about the player's own communication. The user expressed this as naches and Scheißenbedauer, “with only a breath between them.” The exact labels are not the point: the procedure must also support another supplied meaning, an overlapping pair, or a wholly different emotional arc.

**Working interpretation:** the same person may first focus on a learner's achievement, then on a just-sent message they wish they had withheld. Changing the target and appraisal could support a quick shift without undoing the achievement. This is a possible interpretation, not a rule proposal or guaranteed response; the learner's own feeling may differ.

**Useful questions:** what makes each moment meaningful, what cue connects them, and does the experience feel like a reversal, overlap, or tonal confusion? Keep supplied meanings intact and the short interval experiential unless exact timing is needed. Preserve learner agency; do not turn a vocabulary example into a compulsory mentoring mechanic or a communication trap.

This is not an adopted goal for the crate-puzzle example. Specific words, audiences, genres, and social settings can change without changing the framing procedure.

## Evidence and source ownership

Overall emotional intent and the player story belong in the existing concept owner. Relevant local intended experiences can use existing `aesthetics` records; those records do not replace overall intent or require an MDA label. Rules belong in their existing owners. Keep diagrams, verbal emotional arcs, and narrative descriptions readable; no new emotional-state database or mandatory taxonomy fields.

Requested investigation uses existing `experiments` for hypotheses, local measures, methods, observations, interpretation, and limits. No separate measures, decisions, emotion, or player-profile collection. Proposed evidence might combine what happened, what people understood, and how they described the experience, where appropriate and consented. A facial gesture, success count, retry rate, heart rate, or completion time does not uniquely identify a feeling or its object. Missing outward display is not absence of experience.

No framework application, interview, playtest, simulation, game change, goal adoption, or report saving happens automatically. A proposed observation is not an executed investigation. Unknowns may remain open if they do not block the current operation.

## Use with selected frameworks

The shared framing and vocabulary are available without a new framework selection. They clarify supplied intent; they do not authorize running all related framework reviews.

- **Schell, when selected:** Emotion compares intended and current feelings; other relevant cards question a particular rule or player moment. Use precise feelings and alternative interpretations instead of assuming positive/negative is enough.
- **Four Keys, when selected:** examine possible interaction routes and personal/social meanings, including fiero, naches, and mixtures. The Keys are not exhaustive emotion categories.
- **MDA, when selected:** relate rules and behavior to possible experiential responses. Challenge as an appeal is not automatically fiero; social interaction is not automatically naches. Keep concept, local aesthetic, and observed feeling distinct.

These are different explanatory perspectives, not independent votes proving an emotional effect. Use only the selected guide and the detail required for the current question.
