## Purpose and locus

Quantic Foundry asks which gameplay activities people value, how those preferences relate, and how to measure individual differences. Its model grew from survey inventories and statistical grouping, not from a claim that one rule produces one universal pleasure. It can help articulate an audience hypothesis, compare different reasons for choosing an activity, or expose a mismatch between intended appeal and required play.

**Source unit of analysis:** a respondent's pattern of reported preferences across motivations. Aggregated respondent data also support game-audience descriptions when respondents name favorite games. A game does not itself have a psychological profile independent of those respondents.

**Workbench unit of application:** one bounded activity, challenge, rule arrangement, or play sequence, considered against a supplied intention or exploratory question. Trace what players can choose and why that might appeal. The result is an interpretation to investigate, not a measured audience profile or a causal verdict.

Use when a designer wants to distinguish, for example, careful planning from enjoying difficult execution, or experimenting from finishing every objective. Do not use to certify fun, predict sales, infer capability/mastery/emotion, diagnose personality, or decide which design to adopt. Motivation toward challenge is not demonstrated skill at that challenge.

Overall concept/player experience remains broader than this vocabulary. MDA aesthetics are another lens: Quantic Foundry Fantasy or Challenge is not automatically an MDA aesthetic record, even where names overlap. Read the concept itself; do not substitute its `aesthetics` links for intended experience.

## Six clusters, twelve motivations

Use the July 2015 v2 structure, also shown in the captured model-page table. Each cluster groups two related preferences. These are meanings of source labels, not required features or workbench rating scales.

### Action: Destruction and Excitement

- **Destruction:** appeal of wreckage, chaos, weapons, explosions, and spectacular mayhem. This concerns destructive spectacle, not merely having any combat rule.
- **Excitement:** appeal of speed, intensity, surprises, and thrills. A fast interaction may offer this opportunity; its presence does not establish a player's arousal or enjoyment.

Keep them distinct: a hypothetical slow demolition sandbox might offer destruction without sustained time pressure; a hypothetical chase might offer excitement without much destruction. Neither scenario describes the crate puzzle.

### Social: Competition and Community

- **Competition:** appeal of testing oneself against other players through matches, duels, and relative standing.
- **Community:** appeal of interaction, conversation, collaboration, and belonging to a team.

Competition is not equivalent to beating a solitary puzzle or surpassing one's own previous attempt. Community concerns other players, not simply fictional companions. Team competition can support both preferences; their empirical pairing does not require every competitive game to include collaboration.

### Mastery: Challenge and Strategy

- **Challenge:** appeal of demanding skill use, practice, difficult tasks, and overcoming obstacles.
- **Strategy:** appeal of thinking ahead, weighing choices, and careful decision-making in complex situations.

A player may value planning without wanting punishing difficulty. Conversely, difficult execution need not offer much strategic choice. Mastery here names a preference cluster, not proof of acquired competence, a challenge's `tests_mastery` declaration, or a measured learning outcome.

### Achievement: Completion and Power

- **Completion:** appeal of finishing the offered tasks, obtaining collectibles, and finding remaining hidden content.
- **Power:** appeal of becoming stronger within the game world, including character or equipment growth.

Completing everything differs from becoming powerful. Either can make repeated accumulation worthwhile to a player; repetition alone does not identify the motive. A limited energy pool is not automatically a Power appeal: managing scarcity differs from pursuing increased in-world strength.

### Immersion: Fantasy and Story

- **Fantasy:** appeal of inhabiting another identity or place.
- **Story:** appeal of developed plots and interesting characters.

These describe preferences for world/narrative experiences, not a general measurement of absorption or flow. A setting can invite Fantasy without an elaborate plot. Narrative content can be present while a player primarily values the rules and skips the story.

### Creativity: Design and Discovery

- **Design:** appeal of personal expression and substantial customization.
- **Discovery:** appeal of exploring, tinkering, experimenting, and asking what would happen under different actions.

Design means player expression, not the developer's design work. Discovery is broader than walking across a map. Compare trying a rule combination to understand its possibilities with finding the last hidden item to complete a set; similar actions can serve different interests.

## Development, revisions, and relationships

Keep source versions explicit; do not combine their labels into one timeless taxonomy.

- **June 2015 development:** literature review led to about 50 pilot items, tested with 1,127 gamers, primarily MMO players; another 600-person panel replicated results. Exploratory factor analysis produced **five groups and eleven motivations**. The groups were Action, Strategy, Achievement, Social, and Immersion. Strategy contained **Mastery/Planning**; Immersion contained **Customization/Fantasy/Story**. There was no separate Creativity group or established Discovery factor.
- **July 2015 v2:** more than 30,000 English-speaking respondents across regions supported further testing. Separate analyses of US/Canada, Indonesia, and EU samples, and analysis of the full dataset, reportedly yielded the same factor composition. **Discovery** was added; **Customization became Design**, and those two formed **Creativity**. The earlier Strategy cluster became **Mastery**, with **Challenge/Strategy** replacing the earlier **Mastery/Planning** labels. Authors explain that aggregate Strategy reporting had confused users when challenge preference lowered that score. Item weights and norms also changed.
- **Captured model overview:** reports factor analysis from over 140,000 gamers. Its six-column figure retains the v2 twelve-motivation vocabulary. This is a later sample claim, not the sample size of either 2015 study. Publication date is not established; page modification metadata is 2016-10-17.

The June article did not find a coherent Exploration cluster: geographical and mechanical exploration items related to other motivations. July's newly tested Discovery factor is a revision, not evidence that June already contained the later structure. June also deliberately excluded Diversion/Escapism because they describe seeking entertainment more generally, not preferences among gameplay activities. The model therefore does not exhaust reasons people play.

**Hierarchy:** July separates identifying twelve factors from hierarchically clustering their relationships. Its dendrogram joins six close pairs into three broader branches: **Immersion–Exploration**, **Achievement–Mastery**, and **Action–Social**. The first branch contains Immersion and Creativity despite the prose's “Exploration” label. Earlier merging in the dendrogram means greater similarity in that analysis; it is not a player progression or dependency tree.

The model page's two-dimensional map instead labels broad regions **Action–Social**, **Strategy–Achievement**, and **Immersion–Discovery**. It identifies Discovery as a bridge between Immersion and Strategy, and Power between Action–Social and Strategy. Preserve these figure-specific names rather than inventing extra factors or treating the map and dendrogram as identical representations. The map calls itself partial; no axes, distances, or thresholds here license local numerical inference.

**Source tensions:** July's Immersion paragraph says “3 underlying motivations” but lists only Fantasy/Story; the dendrogram and later table support two. June's Social prose prints “Completion” where its meaning and figure say Competition. These are source inconsistencies, not additional motivations. The profile screenshot broadly calls cross-cluster motivations statistically unrelated, while the hierarchy/map discuss broader relationships. Do not turn either simplification into a universal independence claim. Empirical association does not require co-preference in an individual or predict that adding one feature creates another appeal.

## Profiles are not player labels

The original development article explicitly rejects exclusive buckets: each respondent has scores across motivations. A profile is that constellation, not “a Strategy player” or a single dominant identity. Low interest is not a defect; low challenge preference does not mean low ability, and low Social interest does not establish a personality trait.

The model-page report screenshot describes **percentile ranks relative to other profile participants**. A percentile is not the percentage of a person's motivation, probability of liking a game, market share, or a share of playtime. Scores need the actual instrument, model version, reference population, and reporting context. July's revised weights/norms warn against comparing versions as if unchanged.

For this guide, labels remain qualitative vocabulary. Do not reconstruct questionnaire items, estimate scores from a conversation, invent percentiles, or average categories into psychological/fun scores. No full questionnaire, weighting implementation, norms dataset, or interactive report was captured. If actual research results are supplied later, retain their provenance and limits rather than pretending this guide computed them.

## Inputs and authority

Roles: `aesthetics`, `mechanics`, `dynamics`, `challenges`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

Read the concept/mode, selected rules and challenge, intended or observed dynamics, and relevant experiments. Follow only relevant source links into interactions, resources, or `control-vars` when needed to understand choices and constraints. No compulsory category coverage or automatic framework sweep.

Useful inputs are a bounded question, known play context, actual available actions, supplied intention or exploratory lead, and evidence status. Unknown preference data does not block a vocabulary-based discussion. Unknown rules may block a specific activity claim; report that gap rather than filling it.

Keep existing 14-role model. No motivation, skills, measures, or decisions collection follows. Configuration stays in `control-vars`; hypotheses, measures, methods, and observations belong to `experiments`. Guide application authorizes no record edits, investigation execution, design adoption, or mode change. Return chat findings unless report storage is requested under the review contract.

## Apply to a selected design slice

1. **Bound the question and context.** Name activity, rule state, and relevant concept section. In game-design mode compare to supplied direction; in game-explorer mode treat candidate appeal as a lead, not a required destination. Done: explicit scope and intention status, without an invented audience.
2. **Choose useful distinctions.** Select only motivations that clarify the question. State their local meaning: “weighing push consequences” is more useful than “Mastery support.” Keep Challenge and Strategy separate when difficulty versus deliberation matters. Done: each selected term has a reason; excluded terms need no feature proposal.
3. **Trace opportunity through play.** Identify rule, available choice, consequence, and plausible valued activity. Distinguish authored opportunity, predicted behavior, and observed behavior. Done: claim cites actual source sections, not theme or a declared link alone.
4. **Try a competing account.** The same retry might pursue Challenge, Completion, Discovery, or simply escape a confusing state. Check constraints and conflicts: does finite energy encourage planning while discouraging experimentation? Done: at least one credible alternative or boundary is explicit, without claiming preferences are mutually exclusive.
5. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Check experiments for execution and observations. Seek evidence that distinguishes accounts: available choices and voluntary explanations in context, not a motivation diagnosis from one action. Done: evidence status and a possible disconfirming observation are stated. Any proposed investigation remains hypothetical until separately authorized and specified.
6. **Return a bounded artifact.** Report source state, activity–interest interpretation, competing account, evidence gap, and useful next question. Done: reader can see what supports the claim and what would weaken it. Do not end with feature adoption or a measured profile.

## Worked example and review artifact

**Authored sources:** crate-puzzle [concept](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/concept.md), especially Intended player and appeal, Intended experience, Player story, and Constraints and risks; [crate-pushing rule](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/mechanics/crate-pushing.md); [combined room](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/challenges/combined-room.md); [careful-positioning prediction](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/dynamics/careful-positioning.md).

The tutorial is in **game-explorer** mode. Its candidate direction favors considering consequences over fast execution. Successful pushes require a free destination and sufficient energy, then move the crate and spend `push-cost`; blocked/insufficient-energy pushes do neither. Pulling is unavailable. The combined-room description links planning and switch routing but supplies no physical layout. These are authored facts, not proof of interesting choices or audience fit. The concept's player story remains hypothetical, not testimony.

An original review artifact could read:

> **QF-1 — unverified hypothesis; informational; agent interpretation**
>
> Scope: push commitment in the combined room; current tutorial Markdown, not a playable level.
>
> Candidate relation: supports the concept's exploratory planning lead. Strategy is the closest vocabulary match because the proposed activity is considering consequences before a move. Challenge remains a separate possibility only if meaningful difficulty/practice is established.
>
> Rule trace: unavailable pulling and finite push energy constrain recovery; these may make planning valuable. Without geometry, this does not establish a solvable route, meaningful alternatives, or satisfying deliberation.
>
> Counterreading: scarce energy could suppress Discovery by making experimentation feel costly. A retry might instead pursue Completion or resolve confusion.
>
> Evidence: careful positioning is predicted only. The energy/planning playtest has no observations, measures, or executed results. Route-cost is also unexecuted; its mathematical timing reference assumes correct decisions and says nothing about motivation.
>
> Next question: does this constraint create choices worth thinking about, or merely punish trying things? Any later investigation needs its own method and evidence before answering.

**Hypothetical investigation illustration, not game facts or an experiment specification:** if a participant had a choice between continuing a known route and testing a different rule interaction, their voluntary choice and explanation could help distinguish planning, finishing, and curiosity accounts. Optional probing might ask what they wanted to find out and whether they would choose to continue. Choosing a route once would not establish a stable preference profile. Stopping because rules are unclear would weaken an inference of low Challenge/Discovery interest, not confirm it.

The existing [energy/planning experiment](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/energy-encourages-planning.md) and [route-cost experiment](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/route-cost.md) remain unchanged and unexecuted. No investigation design, measurement threshold, or audience classification is adopted by this example.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

- **Planning or difficulty?** Would the intended appeal survive if decisions stayed rich but mistakes became more forgiving? Do not equate lower penalties with less Strategy.
- **Experimenting or exhausting content?** Is the player testing an uncertain interaction, or seeking the last marked objective? What evidence would distinguish Discovery from Completion?
- **Expression or selection?** What can the player meaningfully make their own? A menu choice alone does not establish sustained Design appeal.
- **World or plot?** Does the intended experience depend on inhabiting a role/place, following characters/events, or neither? Do not classify all theme as Story.
- **Power or competence?** Is the sought reward increased in-world strength, or learning to perform better with unchanged capabilities? Do not translate one into the other.
- **Social choice or obligation?** Can interaction be valued for its own sake, or is grouping merely required to obtain another reward? Team participation alone cannot distinguish Community from Power/Completion.
- **Fit or expansion?** Which interests are outside this slice by choice? No checklist requires adding competition, collectibles, story, or customization.

## Evidence, transfer limits, and mistakes

**Empirical basis is not universal validation.** Literature-derived inventory choices and factor analysis support a published preference model, not proof that these are all possible motivations. The June MMO-heavy pilot and later panel are different samples. July's larger dataset consists of English-speaking profile users across regions. Its reported structural replication does not mean equal regional preferences or representative coverage of all players, languages, ages, platforms, and contexts. Detailed linked sample notes and raw data were not read locally; do not invent recruitment precision, reliability coefficients, or population weights.

**Context matters.** Source profiles summarize preferences across games; a local session also includes available choices, familiarity, fatigue, social obligation, and access constraints. These are possible counterexplanations, not diagnosed participant states. One enthusiastic participant cannot establish an audience segment. One person's account can inform that person's reported reason in that context without proving a general cause.

**Commercial audience profiles are descriptive products.** The captured overview links favorite-game nominations to respondent profiles, and its Europa Universalis figure illustrates that aggregation. This is not evidence that a specific mechanic caused those preferences, that every buyer matches the aggregate, or that copying a feature reproduces demand. Do not transfer that game's demographics/percentiles to a tutorial puzzle. The audience dataset, product methodology, and linked reports are outside the captured set.

Avoid these shortcuts:

- Calling a person one player type, treating correlated dimensions as identical, or treating low scores as poor taste/ability.
- Inferring appeal from a title, genre, theme, resource counter, achievement badge, or repeated retry without examining choices and reasons.
- Treating a statistical hierarchy as a feature dependency, learning order, causal mechanism, or mandate to fill six clusters.
- Promoting proposed dynamics, an accepted experiment record, a simulated timing assumption, or the hypothetical player story into observed motivation evidence.
- Turning the source's tentative Action–Social/Extraversion analogy into a personality inference. The workbench does not diagnose extraversion from play.
