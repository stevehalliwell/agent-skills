# Framework index

Choose a lens by the question you need to answer. Read only the selected guide; overlapping entries offer different explanations, not a sequence to run through.

Guides remain provisional. Each guide states its source coverage and limits; four book-based guides still await fuller source-backed expansion. Schell's guide now uses the retained English deck, not a full-book reading. Source text and provenance live in the separate [reference capture index](../references/README.md).

For precise emotional meanings or sequences, use [shared framing](../workflows/emotional-experience.md) and [working emotion vocabulary](../references/emotional-experience/emotions.md). These support the existing frameworks; they are not a nineteenth entry, required intake, or exhaustive taxonomy.

## Rules, systems, and consequences

- **[MDA — Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics](mda.md):** Best for tracing how rules could produce behavior over time and support an intended experience, or working backward from an experience toward supporting rules. Use it when a feature list does not explain why play should feel a particular way.
- **[Game grammar and meaningful play](game-grammar.md):** Best for examining what an action does to a target under different conditions, whether players can perceive the outcome, and why it matters to later choices. Use it to clarify ambiguous interactions, blocked actions, and feedback gaps.
- **[Resource economy](resource-economy.md):** Best for tracing where resources come from, where they go, and how costs, capacities, conversions, and feedback affect available strategies. Use it for scarcity, accumulation, bottlenecks, or disputed sufficiency claims—not a verdict that numerical feasibility means good balance.
- **[Gameplay patterns](gameplay-patterns.md):** Best for recognizing recurring interaction arrangements and comparing ways to realize them. Use it to explain contextual benefits, conflicts, and trade-offs without treating a catalog as a feature shopping list.

## Learning, memory, and support

- **[Skill atoms](skill-atoms.md):** Best for examining what players expect, try, perceive, and learn through an interaction, then how that understanding enables later activity. Use it for learning loops, prerequisite assumptions, feedback breaks, and the difference between reusable loops and one-use arcs.
- **[Theory of Fun](theory-of-fun.md):** Best for asking what pattern a player might learn and what remains interesting as it becomes familiar. Use it to distinguish meaningful practice or recombination from rote repetition and superficial novelty, while leaving room for pleasures other than learning.
- **[Cognitive load](cognitive-load.md):** Best for examining unfamiliar relationships that must be processed together and how presentation, search, guidance, and prior knowledge affect learning. Use it when a task may demand too much coordination or when a demonstration, hint, or staged introduction might help.
- **[Working memory](working-memory.md):** Best for tracing which facts and relationships must remain available during one decision, what can be rechecked, and what depends on learned chunking. Use it for hidden state, transient instructions, interrupted plans, or cue design—not a universal four- or seven-item limit.
- **[ZPD and scaffolding](zpd-scaffolding.md):** Best for examining how help responds to demonstrated understanding, fades, and leaves meaningful responsibility with the learner. Use it to distinguish assisted completion from later independent use; a hint or difficulty gap alone does not establish a developmental zone.

## Experience and motivation

- **[Flow](flow.md):** Best for investigating deep involvement during a particular activity and how perceived demands, capability, goals, and feedback relate to that experience. Use it when familiarity or challenge changes involvement, not to prescribe a universal difficulty sweet spot.
- **[GameFlow](gameflow.md):** Best for reviewing enjoyment conditions across attention, challenge, learning, control, goals, feedback, immersion, and social interaction. Use it for a broader enjoyment-oriented review than psychological Flow, selecting only the conditions relevant to the question.
- **[Self-Determination Theory](self-determination.md):** Best for asking why someone engages, how willingly they engage, and how context supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Use it for pressure, rewards, self-direction, effectiveness, belonging, and reasons for returning—not to classify player types.
- **[Quantic Foundry](quantic-foundry.md):** Best for distinguishing valued gameplay activities, such as planning versus difficult execution or experimentation versus completion. Use its motivation vocabulary to sharpen audience and appeal hypotheses, not infer a measured profile from a feature or one observed action.
- **[Four Keys to Fun](four-keys.md):** Best for tracing possible emotional routes through overcoming obstacles, curiosity, changes in internal experience, and relationships around play. Use it for emotional moments, mixtures, and transitions rather than stable player categories or a requirement to provide every route.

## Social participation and forms of play

- **[Bartle](bartle.md):** Best for examining different interests in a shared virtual world and how participants support or displace one another over time. Use it for participation tensions in its historical MUD context; transfer to other settings cautiously, especially solo games.
- **[Caillois](caillois.md):** Best for discussing competition, chance, make-believe, vertigo, and the tension between improvisation and disciplined rules. Use it to clarify what kind of play an activity offers or constrains, not to require every form or rank one above another.

## Reflection and evidence

- **[Schell — selected deck lenses](schell.md):** Best for reframing a concrete design question through relevant cards, separating source facts from interpretation, and comparing consequences and evidence gaps. Use the retained 116-card reference to select actual lenses; guide includes a worked comparison using Feedback, Punishment, and Emotion rather than a whole-deck review.
- **[Playcentric design](playcentric.md):** Best for turning an intended experience and an unresolved decision into a focused prototype or playtest question. Use it to choose what evidence would distinguish competing explanations and what a result could change, without treating a proposed test as executed evidence.
