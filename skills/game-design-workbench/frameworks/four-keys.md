## Purpose and locus

Use Four Keys to investigate **how an activity might create emotion through play**, not merely whether a game is enjoyable. Lazzaro's original question concerns emotions arising from interaction beyond story cut-scenes: what players perceive, think, do, and experience with other people. Story can still contribute; the lens does not exclude fiction or passive viewing within an experience.

Useful questions include why an obstacle might feel worth overcoming, what invites attention without a win condition, how play changes someone's state, and how other people change an event's meaning. Use when an intended emotional moment lacks a plausible interaction route, or when available accounts suggest different reasons for enjoying the same activity.

**Locus:** a situated player experience, often a moment-to-moment sequence within a session, including anticipation and aftermath. The unit is a player doing or experiencing something in context, not a mechanic label, genre, or permanent kind of player. Extend scope to the room, spectators, communication, and before/after play when relevant. Different people, or one person at different times, can use the same game differently.

Do not use as stable player typing, exhaustive emotion taxonomy, diagnostic test, or proof of audience fit. It does not establish capability, mastery, health benefit, or design quality. It is not a requirement to supply all four routes. Overall concept/player story, Four Keys interpretations, and individual MDA aesthetic intentions remain distinct: similar words do not make them interchangeable models.

## Research basis and terminology

The 2004 report describes contextual observation of favorite games in ordinary play settings, with open-ended questions, post-session questionnaires, video, verbal accounts, and non-verbal cues. Researchers grouped over 2,000 observations into affinity groups and derived several models; Four Keys presents four prominent pathways rather than every result. Cross-genre observation supports asking about different routes, not assuming one genre has one emotional purpose.

Read sample counts carefully. The methodology says 34 adults observed for 90–120 minutes, questionnaires administered to 30 players, over 50 hours of video, and interviews with 15 nearby non-players. The introduction instead describes 15 hardcore and 15 casual gamers plus 15 non-players. Appendix A mentions one ten-year-old; Appendix B includes an age of 57 despite Appendix A's 21–50 description. Preserve these reporting tensions; do not invent a reconciled sample. Most play was solo; group coverage included four same-room console sessions of 3–6 people and two online PC multiplayer sessions.

This is qualitative, contextual research, not a randomized causal test or population prevalence estimate. Appendix A distinguishes study participants from examples based on XEODesign's prior work/opinion and notes some expression photos were taken outside study sessions. The report explicitly excludes causal conclusions about biological, developmental, cultural, or brain-system explanations.

Two retained sources use different presentation vocabularies:

- **2004 report:** The Player / Internal Experience; Hard Fun / Challenge and Strategy; Easy Fun / Immersion; Other Players / Social Experience. This is also its order.
- **Later author overview, publication date unknown:** Hard Fun associates with fiero; Easy Fun with curiosity; Serious Fun with relaxation and excitement; People Fun with amusement. These are prominent associations, not the whole range of feelings in the report.

Use **Serious Fun** as the later label corresponding to the report's internal-experience route, and **People Fun** for its social route. This is a terminology bridge between captures, not evidence that the webpage republishes every original definition unchanged. Neither later label appears as a key heading in the 2004 report. In particular, do not reduce Serious Fun to educational content or a morally serious theme: the later overview explicitly names relaxation and excitement.

## Four routes to emotion

### Hard Fun: pursuing and overcoming meaningful obstacles

The original Challenge and Strategy Key organizes attention around a goal, difficulty, progress, and choices of strategy. Players may enjoy testing an approach, developing it, and seeing effort pay off. **Fiero** means personal triumph over adversity, not any successful action or reward animation. The report distinguishes effortful accomplishment from bare completion; it also describes frustration and boredom as relevant experiences around challenge.

Inspect what makes the objective matter, what obstacle requires effort, what strategies are available, and how feedback makes progress interpretable. Difficulty is relative to a particular player's situation; the label does not grant evidence of that player's ability. Interface obstruction differs from meaningful challenge. The report contrasts rewarding goal pursuit with frustration from navigation or control problems.

Frustration can precede triumph, but is **not required** for fiero in the report's emotion table. Do not prescribe anger or maximum difficulty as ingredients. Nor does sustained concentration prove immediate pleasure; some participants described reward retrospectively.

### Easy Fun: attending, exploring, and experiencing

The original Immersion Key holds attention through interest rather than primarily through a winning condition. Ambiguity, incompleteness, detail, novelty, sensory pleasure, rhythm, and enjoyable actions can invite a closer look. The report emphasizes wonder, awe, and mystery; the later overview foregrounds curiosity. Its table marks mystery as a reported feeling, not technically an emotion under Ekman's classification.

Inspect what someone notices, wonders about, tries, or lingers with for its own sake. A puzzle can invite both figuring-out for interest and solving for accomplishment. The distinction is the role of that activity in the experience, not a binary rule about whether puzzles count.

“Easy” does not mean trivial design, no choices, or a compulsory low difficulty setting. “Immersion” here does not require a 3D world, realism, or forgetting a controller. The report includes shuffling cards, rhythmic actions, playful movement, and exploring systems outside their main objectives. Unclear feedback that prevents action is not automatically productive mystery.

### Serious Fun: changing the internal experience

The 2004 Internal Experience Key asks how play changes what a person feels or thinks during and after play. Prominent associations there are **excitement and relief**; the later overview says **relaxation and excitement**. Examples include clearing one's mind, seeking stimulation, feeling better about playing, and valuing an activity as worthwhile.

Inspect desired state changes and personal appraisal: relief from what, excitement about what, and meaningful to whom? Perception, action, ideas, memories, and social reactions can all contribute. Someone may value an activity's familiar rhythm or believe it exercises their mind without the designer establishing a therapeutic or educational effect.

Personal meaning is one useful application question grounded in the report's cognitive-experience discussion, not a substitute definition for this key. The report's participant health beliefs and “games as therapy” language are accounts of valued experience, not clinical findings. Do not promise restoration, learning transfer, or treatment from this label.

### People Fun: emotion through relationships and shared play

The original Social Experience Key concerns competition, cooperation, teaching, performance, spectacle, and social contact around games. The later overview foregrounds amusement. The report also names **schadenfreude**, pleasure in a rival's misfortune, and **naches**, pleasure or pride in a child or mentee's accomplishment; **kvell** concerns expressing that pride to others. These terms distinguish social meanings that a generic “fun together” label hides.

Inspect who responds to whom, what activity gives their response meaning, and how the response changes subsequent play. A spectator, mentor, rival, or friend can matter without controlling a character. Players may choose a game they otherwise dislike to spend time together. House rules, turn-taking, conversation, and off-screen rituals can form part of the experience.

Mere co-presence or a multiplayer checkbox does not establish connection. Social pressure can worsen a newcomer's experience. The report also discusses caring for characters and simulated social cues; distinguish these from reciprocal human interaction rather than treating them as identical evidence. An intentionally solo slice need not gain multiplayer to satisfy the lens.

## Interaction, mixtures, and transitions

The report describes four contributing aspects of experience: **visceral** reactions to sensory qualities; **behavioral** reactions to using/interacting; **cognitive** reactions to thoughts, memories, and associations; and **social** interaction feeding back into experience. These are not four more Keys or exclusive bins. The report places them under Internal Experience and says the other Keys also use these attributes. Its Easy Fun subsections revisit visceral, cognitive, behavioral, and social immersion.

Trace relationships rather than attach nouns to emotions:

> Situation and prior state; perceived cue; action or attention; response from game/people; player's interpretation; possible emotion; next activity.

This trace is an application aid, not a source equation or computed causal model. Emotion can influence action as well as follow it. A player may change difficulty, invent a challenge, abandon the official objective, or seek friends to obtain a different experience. Avoid assuming every reaction was delivered by a designer's intended sequence.

Routes can overlap or alternate. The report's §3g describes exploration giving way to combat, then victory opening another area to explore. Its social examples show failed coordination becoming shared amusement. Neither transition is guaranteed: a failed attempt can instead end play. The useful question is what contextual change permits a different reading.

Both captures associate best sellers with at least three Keys; the overview says players alternate within one session. Treat this as a source claim motivating examination of variety, **not** a validated success formula, a three-key quota, or an instruction to add all four. Review only the selected slice and relevant routes. A single-key reading can be useful without judging the whole game's coverage.

## Inputs and interpretation

Roles: `dynamics`, `aesthetics`, `interactions`, `experiments`. Read supporting rules and challenge context where needed; no new collection is implied.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, interaction cases, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

Start with the actual concept owner, working mode, selected activity, intended participants/context if supplied, rules and feedback, predicted behavior, and available investigation evidence. In `game-explorer` mode, a possible emotional direction remains a lead, not an adopted target. Missing intent can remain unknown; do not conduct a compulsory four-key interview.

Keep four claim levels explicit:

- **Authored fact or intention:** a rule says what happens, or concept states what is sought. Neither establishes player experience.
- **Framework interpretation:** an agent proposes how the activity could invite a route, with assumptions and alternatives.
- **Observation/account:** an actual event or participant report, tied to source, timing, and context. Neither retry nor completion directly measures a feeling.
- **Conclusion:** what evidence supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved. Missing evidence is not absence of emotion.

Existing 14 roles remain owners. `control-vars` owns named configuration; `experiments` owns investigation hypotheses, local measures, observations, results, and limitations. No separate measures, decisions, or skills collection, required schema, automatic framework sweep, or aggregate fun/psychological score. Guide application authorizes no record edits, experiment execution, or design adoption.

## Apply to a selected slice

1. **Bound the question.** Name an activity and its before/after context. State whether examining an intended experience or exploring a candidate route. Done: scope does not silently expand to the whole game.
2. **Recover the sequence.** Read relevant source owners; separate specified rules from unknown layout, presentation, social setting, and assistance. Done: proposed sequence can be traced to facts, or is clearly hypothetical.
3. **Choose a useful route.** Explain its meaning using the terminology/version above. Trace perception, attention/action, feedback, interpretation, and possible emotion. Done: mechanism is more specific than “it has challenge, therefore Hard Fun.” Leave irrelevant routes out.
4. **Test the interpretation in thought.** Identify assumptions and at least one plausible counterreading or transition. Separate goal difficulty from interface trouble; examine how prior state or other people could change the reading. Done: failure of the preferred interpretation is imaginable without blaming the player.
5. **Compare with intent and evidence.** Relate the route to concept/player story and any individual MDA aesthetic intention without equating them. Check actual observations and their provenance. Done: intended, predicted, and observed claims are distinguishable; unsupported emotion/mastery claims removed.
6. **Return a bounded artifact.** Give source facts, route trace, counterreading, evidence status, and a discriminating question. If later investigation is authorized, its own experiment defines method and measures. Done: human can review the interpretation without approving a design change or data write.

## Worked example and review artifact

**Source facts, not play evidence:** the crate-puzzle [concept](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/concept.md) is an authored tutorial in `game-explorer` mode. Its candidate experience is thoughtful commitment followed by satisfaction when a plan works. Single-player interaction is assumed; no playable build or room geometry exists. Its player story is explicitly hypothetical. [Combined-room](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/challenges/combined-room.md) describes combining planning and switch routing, not an observed solution sequence. [Careful positioning](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/dynamics/careful-positioning.md) is a prediction; [deliberate tension](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/aesthetics/deliberate-tension.md) is one MDA aesthetic intention.

A review could return this artifact in chat or ordinary Markdown; it is not a new record schema:

> **Four Keys review draft — combined-room**
>
> **Kind/origin:** unverified hypothesis / agent interpretation.
>
> **Question:** Could discovering the crate/switch relationship lead into earned accomplishment rather than opaque failure?
>
> **Hypothetical route:** A player notices that placement affects the exit, tries an explanation, revises a plan after an understandable setback, and succeeds through their own effort. Interest in the relationship could be Easy Fun; effortful goal pursuit could be Hard Fun. This is an illustrative sequence, not specified geometry or observed play.
>
> **Assumptions:** Feedback reveals relevant state; a feasible plan exists; the objective matters to this player; retry permits further reasoning. Current documents do not establish these conditions in a playable room.
>
> **Counterreading:** The same outcome could follow copied instructions or accidental moves. Success might yield relief, indifference, or annoyance rather than fiero. Energy scarcity might suppress the exploratory actions that curiosity needs.
>
> **Intent relation:** Possible support for the concept's satisfaction/replanning direction, with possible tension between experimentation and commitment. Deliberate tension alone does not prove either route or the broader experience.
>
> **Evidence status:** No observed emotion or acquired skill. The planning experiment has no defined measures or observations and is unexecuted. Route timing is also unexecuted and assumes correct decisions; its analytical reference is not player evidence.
>
> **Discriminating question:** What did the player think changed at the critical placement, and how did that interpretation affect their next attempt and account of the outcome? Compare explanation, action sequence, and later account rather than treating a smile or completion as proof of triumph.
>
> **Boundary:** No verdict that the design needs People Fun or Serious Fun; no recommendation adopted and no record changed.

The [planning investigation](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/energy-encourages-planning.md) and [route-cost investigation](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/route-cost.md) remain unexecuted. A later authorized study would need its own method, local measures, and explicit handling of alternative accounts. This guide does not supply results or silently extend those experiments.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

These are original workbench prompts, not a reproduced source questionnaire or proprietary instrument. Select those that discriminate the current interpretation.

- **Hard Fun:** What effort could make success personally earned? Countercheck: could the player finish without understanding, or struggle mainly against an unclear interface? What makes this goal matter rather than merely making it difficult?
- **Easy Fun:** What question, sensation, or action is worth lingering with before or outside winning? Countercheck: is ambiguous content inviting exploration, or is essential state simply unreadable?
- **Serious Fun:** What state does this person seek to change, and what makes that change valuable to them? Countercheck: is a designer projecting personal meaning or a health benefit onto someone else's activity?
- **People Fun:** Who notices, helps, competes, performs, or responds, and what changes because of that response? Countercheck: are others a source of amusement, pressure, exclusion, distraction, or some mixture?
- **Transition:** What event could shift attention from exploring to overcoming, or from failure to laughter? Countercheck: what would prevent that shift or lead the person to stop instead?
- **Evidence:** When did the reaction occur, and what did the person say it concerned? Countercheck: consider relief at stopping, a friend's remark, a prior mood, or an unrelated event. Allow unexpected feelings and no reported feeling.

## Common mistakes and transfer limits

- Do not assign permanent “Hard Fun players” from one episode or assume the Keys exhaust motives. Mood, setting, relationships, and stage of play can change the route.
- Do not treat emotion words as rule properties: a difficult puzzle is not itself triumph; a beautiful scene is not itself wonder. Specify the player/context bridge and keep it provisional.
- Do not equate negative emotion with failure or prescribe frustration as a route to enjoyment. Check context, duration, and what follows. The report itself distinguishes goal obstacles from interface problems.
- Do not infer emotion from a single gesture. Report §4b explicitly identifies a raised arm as touching a bumped head, not fiero. Even the source's confident facial-expression interpretations do not warrant automatic emotion detection in the workbench.
- Do not infer weak solo experience from fewer outward displays. The report describes concentrated play with little expression and more intense displays in co-located groups; display frequency is not a calibrated enjoyment scale.
- Keep the report's descriptive participant accounts separate from practitioner opinion and its broader psychological claims. This guide does not independently validate its claims about universal gestures, cognition, addiction, or therapeutic effects.
- Transfer to another age group, culture, accessibility context, genre, platform, remote group, or productivity tool remains an application hypothesis. The later overview claims productivity use but supplies no method or evaluation data in the retained article.

Both substantive captures were read, not just source cards. This does not establish framework completeness. The report conversion has character corruption, imperfect captions/read order and table layout; no page-perfect audit or OCR was performed. Selected figures were visually checked: the frustration-to-fiero example (§2c), the raised-arm counterexample (§4b), and the overview thumbnail. The thumbnail shows later four-key labels arranged around player experience, but fine print is too small for reliable extraction; do not derive additional categories or formal relationships from it.
