## Scope

Explore possible routes to emotion through challenge/triumph, curiosity, meaningful engagement, and social amusement.

## Inputs and query support

Roles: `dynamics`, `aesthetics`, `interactions`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, interaction cases, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

## Working concepts

Vocabulary: Hard Fun, Easy Fun, Serious Fun, People Fun are Lazzaro’s labels. They are not synonyms for MDA aesthetics or Koster’s learning account.

- **Hard Fun:** Use challenge and earned overcoming as one possible route to emotion. Difficulty alone does not establish triumph; the player’s understanding, investment, and interpretation matter.
- **Easy Fun:** Use curiosity, exploration, and playful discovery as another route. Easy here should not be read as a universal demand for low challenge or the absence of meaningful decisions.
- **Serious Fun:** Ask about intended personal meaning or change in experience, such as purposeful engagement or restoration. Keep any claimed emotional effect contextual and unverified without relevant evidence.
- **People Fun:** Ask what participants do together that might create shared amusement or connection. Mere co-presence is not an established emotional mechanism, and intentionally solo play need not cover this route.

## Apply to selected slice

1. Choose an intended emotional moment or short activity. State which key is useful to that question; do not treat all four as compulsory targets for the whole game.
2. Read the relevant rules, dynamic hypotheses, interactions, and experience intentions. Describe the sequence that could produce the emotion, including anticipation and aftermath where source supports them.
3. Separate intended feeling from player action and reported reaction. Name assumptions about understanding, stakes, discovery, or social context that connect the sequence to the intended emotion.
4. Consider one different emotional reading or transition. The same obstacle can invite triumph, relief, annoyance, or disengagement under different conditions; keep unsupported readings hypothetical.
5. Return the proposed emotional route, supporting facts, and a discriminating question. Do not score emotions, require every key, or adopt presentation or challenge changes during review.

## Original prompts

1. What sequence could make overcoming this obstacle feel like earned triumph?
2. What invites curiosity before the player knows the solution?
3. What could make the activity personally meaningful or emotionally restorative in this intended context?
4. If people play together, what shared activity could create amusement rather than merely co-presence?
5. How could the emotional tone change within a session without losing the main intention?
6. Which described player reaction would support or contradict the proposed emotional route?

## Worked example

- **Authored facts:** Combined-room brings together push planning and switch routing. The tutorial records no reactions to success or discovery.
- **Interpretation:** Understanding the switch relationship could invite curiosity and solving the combination could invite triumph. The events and emotional interpretations are distinct claims.
- **Counterreading:** A solution reached by copying could feel different from a self-derived plan. An unclear failure could change anticipation into confusion; neither response is established by the current source.
- **Useful next question:** Which moment should change the emotional tone, and what account or behavior would support that interpretation without treating a smile, retry, or completion as a direct emotion measurement?

## Evidence and counterchecks

- Relate any observed reaction to its timing and context. The reaction after a solution may concern relief, social response, or an unrelated event.
- Keep curiosity, triumph, meaning, and shared amusement distinguishable. They can overlap without becoming interchangeable labels for general enjoyment.
- A later authorized observation should permit unexpected reactions. Evidence collection that only asks whether the intended emotion occurred can hide competing interpretations.

## Common traps

- Do not translate Hard Fun into maximum difficulty or Easy Fun into trivial mechanics.
- Do not equate these keys with MDA aesthetics or Koster’s learning account; their purposes and vocabulary differ.
- Do not treat absent social play or unrecorded emotion as a failed category score.

## Missing data and evidence limits

Keep undeclared social experience inapplicable when appropriate. Do not treat missing observed emotion as evidence of absent emotion.

No category quota, emotional score, copied questionnaire, or rule that every game needs every key. Links to emotional categories remain interpretations until supported by relevant evidence.
