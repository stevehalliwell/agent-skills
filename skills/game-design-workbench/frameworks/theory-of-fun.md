## Purpose and locus

Use this lens to ask **what relationship a player might learn, how play makes it learnable, and what remains interesting as that relationship becomes familiar**. It helps distinguish a new problem from a new-looking instance, useful practice from repeated proof, and an apparent lack of novelty from a mismatch with prior knowledge.

Koster's 2005 essay argues that games deserve serious attention as media for learning through simplified models. Fun, in this account, rewards learning patterns; game design can extend interest by offering patterns worth continued interpretation. This is an explanatory proposal and design provocation, not a validated diagnostic instrument.

**Locus:** the relationship between a game's rule-governed model and a player's developing understanding over encounters. A useful unit of analysis is one decision relationship across a small progression: initial encounter, opportunities to practise, then a changed situation. One move may reveal feedback but be too narrow to show learning. An entire game's retention is usually too broad for a supported local claim.

A challenge route describes offered opportunities, not a player's internal model. The same room can present a discovery to one person and a familiar exercise to another. Describe assumed prior knowledge explicitly; do not infer capability or mastery from route position, rank, or completion.

Use for:

- Reviewing whether a puzzle sequence asks for new reasoning or merely repeats a known procedure.
- Examining how understandable consequences let players revise predictions.
- Exploring whether familiar operations support deeper combinations or interpretation.
- Questioning a claim that additional content will restore interest.

Do not use as a universal explanation of pleasure, a player-type taxonomy, or a fun score. It cannot alone diagnose disengagement, set difficulty, prove transfer outside a game, or decide that a design should change. Concept/player-story intent remains the comparison point in design mode. Individual MDA aesthetics are separate intentions, not synonyms for the whole experience or Koster's learning account. In explorer mode, a candidate learning relationship is a lead, not a missing requirement to repair.

## Koster's account: models, practice, and changing interest

The following concepts paraphrase the captured articles. Their connections matter more than filling categories. They are not stages that every player must traverse in a fixed order.

### Patterns, mental models, and chunking

In the 2005 essay, pattern-matching organizes incoming information into usable regularities. **Chunking** makes a familiar activity available as a compact, routine operation rather than a fresh reconstruction of every step. Koster uses everyday driving and dressing to explain this compression. A mental model is useful because it helps someone deal with a situation without treating each detail as unrelated noise.

Games supply a second sense of model: rules and tokens simplify some relationships into an abstract, manipulable setting. The game model is not the same as the player's understanding of it. A player can form an incomplete or mistaken account of a consistent game rule.

Workbench adaptation: write the proposed pattern as a relationship with consequences, such as “moving this object changes both access and the condition holding an exit open.” “Push,” “three rooms,” and “a new skin” name an action, quantity, and presentation; none alone names the relationship to understand. Do not use chunking as an inferred memory capacity or claim that an action has become automatic without evidence.

### Practice is not the same as first recognition

Koster distinguishes noticing a pattern from making it routine through practice. The account therefore does not imply that a second encounter is wasted. Repeated encounters can establish reliability, allow prediction, or free attention for another relationship.

The 2008 post adds a crucial distinction: learning isolated material by rote can leave the underlying system opaque. A player might memorize a move sequence without understanding why it works. Conversely, repeated use of a familiar move can support a new strategic problem. Count neither repetitions nor mechanics as a proxy for learning depth.

Workbench adaptation: distinguish three possible descriptions, using only those relevant to the slice:

- **Recognition:** a relationship becomes a plausible explanation of what happened.
- **Practice:** varied or repeated use lets the player check and use that relationship.
- **Recombination or transfer:** a changed situation asks the player to choose how familiar relationships apply together.

These are review distinctions, not Koster's published category instrument, record statuses, or proven player states. Transfer here means a proposed use across game situations unless evidence supports a wider claim.

### Feedback has two meanings here

The 2005 essay calls fun the brain's positive feedback for learning. That explanatory claim is not identical to interface feedback such as a state change, sound, or explanation after an action. Nor does a visible reward establish that learning occurred.

For application, inspect action consequences and perceptible feedback because players need some basis for testing a mental model. Ask whether competing explanations of an outcome can be distinguished. A blocked push without a clear reason may hide a consistent rule; the problem may be legibility rather than a lack of patterns. This prediction/action/consequence review is a workbench adaptation, not an additional loop diagram claimed from these articles.

### Changing interest, routine, and boredom

The 2005 argument links mastery of an underlying pattern with erosion of fun. It proposes self-refreshing gameplay: patterns that remain complex and open to interpretation. Other players are presented as a rich source of changing patterns; the essay also challenges designs that permit only one solution or interpretation.

The 2008 post makes the local fit more explicit. Koster contrasts operating near a challenge boundary or experiencing mastery as power with repeatedly proving an already mastered analytical procedure. He also distinguishes seeing a system from learning disconnected material that still feels like noise. A promise of future fun does not make the present activity fun.

Use these distinctions to form alternatives, not classify a player automatically:

- Relationship may still be opaque: outcome supplies insufficient basis for a useful model.
- Relationship may be familiar but still worth practising or using fluently.
- Familiar operation may now support a less familiar higher-level decision.
- Repeated task may offer no further interpretation relevant to the intended experience.

These are possible readings of a situation, not measured boredom states. Lack of novelty is not evidence of boredom. Increasing complexity can obscure a pattern rather than renew interest.

## Reading the two accounts together

The 2005 essay makes broad rhetorical claims: fun is the reward for learning; mastered patterns eventually become boring; designers should extend cognitive challenge. The 2008 response resists treating “fun” as one unambiguous term. It distinguishes game-related learning pleasure from visceral or physical responses and describes models of fun as complementary starting points, not definitive answers.

Treat this as qualification and emphasis across two texts, not a formally versioned replacement theory. The later post does not abandon mental models; it sharpens why rote learning, individual starting points, and other pleasures complicate the earlier formulation. Its opening quotation is another writer's challenge, not Koster's own definition. Its discussion of LeBlanc's eight kinds is Koster's comparison, not an eight-part Theory of Fun taxonomy to apply here.

Practical consequence: retain a learning hypothesis alongside alternatives such as fluent enjoyment, atmosphere, expression, social company, or sensory pleasure. These alternatives are workbench counterchecks, not extra Koster categories. A learning account that explains every outcome after the fact without a possible counterexample is not a useful local investigation.

## Inputs and interpretation

Roles: `skill-atoms`, `challenges`, `mechanics`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: skill atoms, challenge routes, and experiment evidence.

Read the current concept/mode and selected authored slice first. Use existing role owners; no new collection or required schema follows from this guide.

- **Skill atoms:** understanding, expression, learning loop, evidence sought, and prerequisites describe intended learning. A prerequisite link does not establish acquired knowledge; `tests_mastery` elsewhere is also intent, not a result.
- **Challenges:** compare what is introduced, practised, required, and offered next. Read body descriptions and route/assistance context. Co-present labels alone do not show that ideas interact.
- **Mechanics and relevant cases:** establish actual state transitions, conditions, and consequences. Follow configuration references to `control-vars` when necessary; do not invent values, layouts, or executable semantics.
- **Experiments:** distinguish hypotheses, measures, expectations, observations, results, and interpretation. All investigation data belongs here; no separate measures, decisions, or skills collection. Accepted intent or accepted hypothesis is not empirical support.
- **Missing inputs:** absent observations permit a design-level hypothesis only. Missing geometry may prevent judging whether a proposed relationship is encountered at all. Report this limit rather than treating missing information as a teaching failure.

A review can stay in chat or ordinary Markdown. Guide application does not authorize record edits, experiment execution, design adoption, or automatic framework sweeps. Follow the [selected-review contract](../workflows/review.md) if a report is requested; findings need traceable source, interpretation, consequence/question, and limits.

## Apply to a selected slice

1. **Bound the question.** Select one decision relationship and a short sequence where it may change. State actual mode and supplied intent or candidate direction. Done: question is narrower than “is this fun?”
2. **Describe the game model.** State relevant rule relationships and what consequences are available to inspect. Mark missing feedback, rules, or geometry. Done: facts are separated from assumptions about a possible encounter.
3. **Propose contrasting player models.** For example, hypothetically knowing a switch condition differs from knowing how crate placement can preserve it while allowing further movement. Include an already knowledgeable perspective when useful. Done: expected difference is an explicit prediction or choice, not an inferred capability label.
4. **Trace encounter, practice, and changed use.** Ask what could reveal the relationship, how a player could test it, and what a later instance requires beyond reproducing the same sequence. Do not require all three functions in every challenge. Done: distinguish a new relationship from new presentation or quantity, with reasons.
5. **Examine interest without prescribing novelty.** Explain what repetition might support and what remains open to interpretation. Consider opaque noise, rote success, fluent enjoyment, and genuine new understanding as alternatives. Done: at least one plausible competing account remains visible.
6. **Specify a discriminating observation.** Suggest what a future investigation would need to distinguish those accounts, including contrary evidence and context. Measures/hypotheses/observations, if later retained, belong in an experiment and need their own definitions. Done: no invented participant data, psychological score, or causal verdict.
7. **Return a bounded artifact.** Cite authored support, state interpretation and evidence status, offer a question or proposed comparison, and retain source gaps. Done: reader can decide what needs investigation without mistaking a recommendation for adoption.

Review both rather than inventing a complete relational trace.

### Hypothetical interpretation, not a room specification

A possible pattern is that crate placement must satisfy a switch condition while preserving a useful future position. That relationship could connect two familiar ideas instead of simply placing them in sequence. No supplied map establishes that such a choice exists or is necessary. Limited energy might add meaningful planning, but it might also discourage the experimentation through which someone discovers a relationship.

A hypothetical player might first predict “reaching the switch is enough,” then revise that prediction after inspecting a later constraint. Another might recognize the relevant relationship immediately. Neither story is an observation. Immediate success would not establish that the design offers no learning to anyone; repeated failure would not establish productive discovery.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

Use questions suited to the selected relationship, not a compulsory checklist:

- What could the player predict after this encounter that they could not predict before? Could they have known it already?
- What outcome would reveal that their current model is wrong? Can feedback distinguish that outcome from an unrelated rule?
- If positions, appearance, or numbers change, which causal relationship stays the same? Does the proposed change demand a new decision?
- Does success require understanding, or can a fixed recipe produce it? When is fluent performance itself intended?
- What uncertainty remains after the basic operation becomes routine? Is that uncertainty interpretable or merely arbitrary?
- Does the next encounter allow useful testing, or punish exploration before consequences can be understood?
- If learning apparently increases while reported enjoyment decreases, what part of the local explanation would need revision?
- What would make us keep this repetition: consolidation, expression, rhythm, comfort, or a different intention? Which of these is supplied intent versus a proposed rationale?

Look for converging evidence, not one diagnostic behavior. Changed predictions and choices across situations can support a local learning interpretation. Faster completion can instead reflect memorization, easier geometry, or motor familiarity. A player's account can support an experience claim while leaving its cause unresolved. Repetition, retries, hesitation, or abandonment alone cannot establish boredom, mastery, or emotion.

## Common mistakes and transfer limits

- **Equating novelty with learning:** random variation may be noise. Name a relationship that can be understood and used.
- **Equating completion with mastery:** success can follow hints, chance, or a remembered sequence. Preserve how success occurred and what evidence remains missing.
- **Treating difficulty as depth:** more simultaneous mechanics may hide relationships. Do not add complexity solely because a pattern is familiar.
- **Treating all repetition as a defect:** practice and fluent use can be valuable. Ask what the repetition serves relative to actual intent.
- **Reading brains from documents:** authored learning loops, prerequisite links, and challenge routes are offers, not observations of mental states.
- **Turning a lens into a mandate:** Koster's self-refreshing-play argument does not require multiplayer, endless content, indefinite retention, or multiple solutions in every local puzzle. A finite experience can intentionally end after understanding develops.
- **Claiming outside transfer:** understanding a game abstraction does not establish real-world competence or educational benefit. The 2005 essay's claims about games teaching serious subjects motivate questions; these captures supply no local evaluation protocol or empirical proof for this design.
