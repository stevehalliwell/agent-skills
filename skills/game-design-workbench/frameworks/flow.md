## Purpose and locus

Use Flow to investigate how a person becomes deeply involved in an activity that feels worth doing for its own sake. Challenge relative to capability is one relationship in that account, not its entire purpose. The author’s 2004 talk starts from meaningful, rewarding experience across work, art, sport, and everyday life; it is not a game-difficulty tuning manual.

**Unit of analysis:** a person doing a particular activity during a bounded episode, with a particular history of familiarity. In a game, select an encounter, attempt, or transition within an encounter. A route or mechanic can supply conditions for that episode; neither is itself a psychological state. A whole-game label hides changes between learning, fluent action, interruption, and recovery.

Use this lens when asking whether goals, feedback, and demands could support sustained involvement, or why experience might change with practice. Keep separate:

- **Authored conditions:** rules, cues, task demands, and opportunities offered by design.
- **Perceived conditions:** what this person understands the goal, demand, and available capability to be.
- **Reported state:** their account of concentration, fluency, self-consciousness, control, or time experience.
- **Performance:** observed task outcomes under stated rules and context.
- **Enjoyment:** an evaluative experience that can occur with or without the other features of Flow. A positive rating alone does not establish Flow.

Flow is not a required target for every moment. Deliberate reflection, ambiguity, rest, social interruption, or discomfort may serve supplied intent. In `game-design` mode compare with concept intent; in `game-explorer` mode explore a candidate experience without treating missing destination as a defect. Overall concept/player story is not an individual MDA aesthetic. Do not translate the brief into an aesthetic record or assume “challenge” as an MDA intention establishes Flow.

This guide is an independently authored workbench application, provisional pending human review. Source distinctions below matter more than a single preferred diagram. It does not authorize record edits, experiment execution, or design adoption.

## Conditions and experienced state

Cowley et al. (2019), Introduction, explicitly distinguish three antecedent conditions from six experiential features. This is a useful analytical separation, not a compulsory form to fill or a guarantee that conditions produce the state.

**Conditions:**

1. **Demanding challenge relative to skill.** The source condition is a match between task demands and skill in a demanding activity: something difficult but experienced as possible, not merely a high authored difficulty number. Capability concerns this task now; it is not a stable player trait inferred from rank or success.
2. **Clear, personally significant goals.** The person has an immediate direction and reason to pursue it. Knowing what to attempt need not mean knowing the solution. A distant objective without a legible next concern may not orient current action.
3. **Unambiguous feedback on progress.** Results let the person relate action to goal and adjust. Feedback can come from the activity itself, not only explicit messages or rewards. A state transition written in a rule is not proof that its presentation is noticeable or understood.

**Features of experience, paraphrased from that article:**

- **Present-focused concentration:** attention stays with the current activity.
- **Action and awareness merge:** doing and attending feel integrated rather than separate monitoring tasks.
- **Reduced reflective self-consciousness and effortlessness:** less attention goes to evaluating oneself; this does not mean the task demands no effort or learning.
- **Sense of personal control:** the person feels able to act effectively. This is experiential confidence, not proof of perfect control over outcomes.
- **Positive affect/enjoyment:** doing the activity feels rewarding.
- **Altered time experience:** time may feel faster or slower than ordinary clock time.

Together these features support the account of an **autotelic** activity: doing it is rewarding in itself, not only a means to an external prize. They are related descriptions, not independent design sliders or an aggregate psychological/fun score.

The talk’s discussion at 14:18–15:03 calls its cluster “seven conditions,” mixing focus, clarity, feasibility despite difficulty, changed self/time experience, and intrinsic reward. Captions do not retain the slide’s seven labels. Do not fabricate an exact seven-item instrument from this spoken passage or silently equate it with the article’s three-plus-six distinction.

### Attention, goals, and feedback act together

At 08:09–11:05 the talk uses a trained composer to explain how absorbed activity can leave little attention for bodily concerns or self-monitoring. Later examples extend beyond composition. The practical implication is to inspect what occupies attention and how learned technique supports fluent action, not to demand automaticity from a first encounter.

Clear immediate goals and interpretable feedback can keep attention on the activity: attempt something, recognize its effect, adjust. Unclear feedback may instead force attention onto deciphering the interface. Conversely, a puzzle can retain uncertainty about its solution while making action consequences clear. Distinguish useful thought about the puzzle from accidental work identifying what the game just did.

The talk’s numeric information-processing estimates and claim about years of creative training are not adopted as workbench limits or prerequisites. They do not justify a timer, fixed training period, or cognitive-capacity calculation for a game.

## Challenge, capability, and changing reference levels

There is no single source-settled universal sweet-spot curve. Cowley et al., Introduction, compare models with materially different assumptions:

- **Original channel model (1975, as discussed in the 2019 article):** matching challenge and skill can support Flow at different absolute levels. This is not evidence that any easy task automatically produces Flow.
- **Quadrant/octant models:** matching is insufficient if both challenge and skill are low; Flow requires levels above a reference. What supplies that reference is consequential: personal everyday average, task history, population norm, or another standard are not interchangeable.
- **Intensity model (Keller and Landhäußer, 2012, as discussed there):** perceived balance and subjective task value replace reference levels, but the model does not retain the direction of imbalance. The article questions whether that loss matters.

These earlier works were not independently read for this guide. Their descriptions here are attributed to Cowley et al.’s comparison, not presented as full coverage of each original model.

### The author talk’s person-relative map

At 15:03–16:03 Csikszentmihalyi describes experience sampling: pagers prompt reports of current activity, feelings, location, thoughts, experienced challenge, and felt skill ten times daily. Each person’s mean challenge and skill locate that person’s diagram center. The talk places Flow where both are above that person’s average, not at a universal game difficulty value.

At 16:25–17:44 it describes neighboring experiences and possible movement:

- **Arousal:** over-challenged relative to current skills; developing skill can support movement toward Flow.
- **Control:** comfortable but no longer very challenging; taking on more challenge can support movement toward Flow.
- **Relaxation:** a non-Flow experience the talk still describes as acceptable.
- **Boredom and apathy:** increasingly negative experiences in the account; apathy involves little challenge or use of skill.

These are source experience categories, not diagnoses available from a route graph. The full slide geometry and labels are absent from captions; do not reconstruct a complete octant map from memory. Nor does the talk prescribe eliminating control or relaxation from a designed experience.

Experience sampling grounds the model in repeated person-relative reports. A single post-session opinion, authored rank, or designer’s impression is not equivalent to that sampling method. In-game prompts can also interrupt the absorption being studied; sampling frequency and boundaries need an explicit investigation method, not automatic reuse of “ten times daily.”

### What the 2019 learning study adds—and leaves open

Cowley et al. studied nine adults playing a novel high-speed steering task for 40 trials across eight sessions over 2–3 weeks. Speed increased during play and fell after collisions; avoiding obstacles therefore supported faster completion and increasing demand. Clear task feedback and adaptive demand were deliberately built in. Figures 1–2 show task and repeated-trial protocol; this was not a spatial puzzle progression study.

The researchers collected post-trial Flow Short Scale reports. They analyzed its fluency and absorption core separately from perceived importance; this guide does not reproduce the instrument or prescribe its use. Physiology was recorded in some sessions but is not analyzed in this article, so the paper supplies no physiological Flow detector.

Performance improved and individual power-law learning curves fit the data. Mean reported Flow stayed elevated rather than reliably increasing with overall improvement. At trial level, better-than-model-predicted performance was associated with higher reported Flow; worse-than-predicted performance with lower reports. Figure 3 distinguishes the learning trend from deviations around it; Figures 4–5 show why improvement and increasing Flow cannot be treated as the same result.

“Expected” here means a fitted statistical prediction from performance history, not a measured belief held by the participant. Do not infer subjective expectations from residuals, or turn authored skill ranks into learning curves. The reported association does not determine whether Flow improved performance, easier random obstacle layouts helped both, or successful outcomes influenced retrospective reports. Participants saw results before reporting. The authors discuss all three explanations and cannot conclusively select one.

The study challenges a **fixed-reference** reading of above-average challenge/skill during learning. It does not disprove the talk’s person-relative account or establish a universal replacement: everyday sampling and a within-task learning experiment have different reference contexts. The article also questions ignoring the direction of imbalance. Keep both reference level and direction explicit rather than merging these versions into “always balance difficulty exactly with skill.”

Workbench adaptation: describe demand/capability relationships as time- and task-specific hypotheses. Compare first encounter, developing familiarity, and repeat play where relevant. No demand increase, difficulty reduction, or adaptive system follows automatically from this lens.

## Inputs and interpretation

Roles: `skill-atoms`, `challenges`, `mechanics`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: skill atoms, challenge routes, and experiment evidence.

Read selected concept and relevant source bodies, not just links or query columns. Use existing [model owners](../workflows/model.md); preserve all 14 roles. Where relevant, inspect interactions for case feedback and `control-vars` for named configuration. No new skills, measures, decisions, or player-profile collection is needed.

- **Challenge routes** identify authored opportunities and alternatives, not proven reachability or acquired capability.
- **Skill atoms** describe declared understanding, expression, learning opportunities, and prerequisites. Authored rank is conceptual placement, not a calibrated ability estimate; `tests_mastery` is intent, not measured mastery.
- **Mechanics and cases** establish what changes after action. Their presentation, legibility, and perceived relevance may remain unknown.
- **Experiments** own hypotheses, local measure definitions, observations, results, provenance, and limitations. An accepted investigation with no observations supplies no empirical support.
- **Concept/player story** supplies candidate or adopted intent according to its actual status. A hypothetical story remains hypothetical, not a participant account.

Keep three kinds of uncertainty separate: missing design specification, untested experience hypothesis, and source-model disagreement. More detailed design records can resolve the first without resolving the others. Query support can inventory declared routes or records; it cannot calculate Flow.

## Apply to a selected episode

1. **Bound episode and reason for this lens.** Name activity, start/end concern, route/context, and relevant intent. State why absorption matters here—or that suitability itself is the question. Done: a local question, not a whole-game Flow verdict.
2. **Trace the attention loop.** Identify immediate goal, choice/action, visible result, and next adjustment from actual design facts. Mark unspecified presentation separately from absent rules. Done: explain what the player could attend to without pretending they have understood it.
3. **Describe demands and assumed capability.** Name what must be noticed, understood, coordinated, or executed. Contrast a less familiar and a more familiar perspective only as hypotheses. Include prior exposure, assistance, recovery, and voluntary challenge when documented. Done: assumptions are explicit; no inferred mastery or numeric balance curve.
4. **Compare across time.** Ask what changes after learning, failure, route choice, or a repeat attempt. Specify reference when saying “higher,” “easier,” or “better than expected.” Done: static authored difficulty is not mistaken for stable experience.
5. **Separate condition claims from state claims.** State what rules support, what remains untested, and at least one competing explanation for apparent involvement. Done: success, persistence, enjoyment, and reported absorption remain distinct.
6. **Return a bounded artifact.** Include source anchors, demand/capability hypothesis, goal/feedback issue, alternative reading, and useful next investigation. An ordinary review note is enough; use [review contract](../workflows/review.md) if saving was requested. Done: reader can dispute reasoning without guessing evidence or adoption status.
7. **If evidence is needed, propose—not execute—an investigation.** Distinguish behavior observations from voluntary accounts of experience. Consider first/repeat attempts and recall/prompt disruption. Any local measures, hypotheses, and observations belong within an authorized experiment, with boundaries and undefined values defined there. Done: no fabricated trials, results, psychological scores, or source edits.

## Worked example and review artifact

**Selected slice:** optional-practice bypass into combined-room in the crate-puzzle example. This is a documentation review, not playtesting or a claim that a playable layout exists.

**Authored facts checked:**

- [Concept](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/concept.md), Intended experience and Player story: mode is `game-explorer`; candidate direction favors thoughtful commitment, understandable consequences, and satisfaction from replanning, not fast execution. Player story is explicitly hypothetical.
- [Push-room](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/challenges/push-room.md), `next`: leads to switch-practice or directly to combined-room.
- [Switch-practice](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/challenges/switch-practice.md), `introduces` and `next`: declares switch reading/routing opportunities before combined-room. This does not prove learning.
- [Combined-room](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/challenges/combined-room.md), `requires` and body: combines planning, switch routing, and coordination; prior planning support is not documented. No physical room map is supplied.
- [Switch-exit](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/mechanics/switch-exit.md): crate occupancy opens exit; removal closes it. Presentation and closed-exit walking behavior are not settled by that rule.
- [Restart](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/mechanics/challenge-restart.md): reconstructs starting arrangement. Missing challenge links do not prove restart unavailable; concept leaves its discovery/assistance unspecified.

**Hypothetical interpretation:** someone unfamiliar with switch routing could arrive through the bypass without a clear account of how crate placement affects the exit. Attention might go to deciphering the rule rather than planning with it. Someone already familiar could instead welcome immediate coordination. Neither route implies anxiety, boredom, capability, or Flow. Slower planning could support the candidate experience rather than count as poor performance.

A useful output could be this review note, not a new record schema:

> **F-1 — Unknown/unresolved; agent interpretation; experience unverified.** Severity: investigation question, not an established defect.
>
> Scope: push-room bypass into first combined-room attempt. Source state: current working example documents, not an executed build.
>
> Evidence: route permits bypass; combined-room declares switch-routing demands; switch-exit defines state change but not its presentation. No room layout or player observations establish legibility.
>
> Hypothesis: if the crate/exit relationship is not apparent, an unfamiliar player may spend attention discovering that relationship rather than forming a plan. Familiar players may not have that problem.
>
> Intent relation: unknown against candidate thoughtful commitment; discovery could support that direction, while opaque consequences could obstruct it.
>
> Proposed investigation: in an authorized playable test, examine what participants think opens the exit, which result they use to revise a plan, and their voluntary account of involvement. Compare relevant prior familiarity and route context. Do not substitute completion time for understanding or absorption.
>
> Alternative to discuss: optional practice, clearer consequence feedback, or retaining intentional discovery. No route change adopted.
>
> Limits: conceptual routes only; no empirical experience claim, difficulty calibration, or mastery finding.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

- **Goal:** Can someone name the current concern without already knowing the solution? Countercheck: is confusion about where to go being mistaken for interesting uncertainty about how to solve it?
- **Feedback:** Which consequence can inform the next choice while it still matters? Countercheck: does the rule describe a change the player might never notice?
- **Attention:** Is thought directed toward the intended activity or toward interpreting hidden state/interface behavior? Countercheck: silence, long sessions, and repeated attempts also fit confusion or reluctant persistence.
- **Capability:** What specific prior understanding does this demand assume, and what evidence supports that assumption? Countercheck: reaching a node, accepting a skill record, or completing one attempt does not establish it.
- **Learning:** Could the same encounter become comfortable after repetition, or reveal deeper self-selected goals? Countercheck: neither familiarity nor faster completion guarantees more Flow.
- **Reference:** Higher challenge compared with whose prior experience, which task, and which episode? Countercheck: do not mix personal reports, authored rank, and population comparisons on one axis.
- **Experience evidence:** What would distinguish fluent involvement from successful but effortful compliance? Countercheck: collect participant accounts when authorized; behavior alone cannot resolve that distinction.
- **Intent:** Would restoring concentration improve this particular moment? Countercheck: reflection, rest, or discomfort may be wanted; Flow is not automatically preferable.

## Common mistakes and transfer limits

- **Turning conditions into a diagnosis.** Clear goals and feedback are inspectable design claims, not proof that someone entered Flow. Missing practice is not measured anxiety.
- **Treating balance as exact equality.** Sources disagree about level, reference, value, and direction. A universal hump or diagonal sweet spot conceals those differences.
- **Equating learning with Flow or performance with enjoyment.** The 2019 study separates overall learning from trial-relative associations. Correlation supplies no causal design prescription or universal learning benefit.
- **Substituting GameFlow.** GameFlow is a separate game-enjoyment evaluation model, not another name for this experiential account. Its categories or scores cannot fill gaps in Flow evidence. Load it only if separately selected; no automatic framework sweep.
- **Importing a study protocol as product design.** An adaptive steering task with a small convenience sample does not validate compulsory adaptation, timed pressure, or faster play for a deliberate puzzle. Repeated measurements improve within-person detail but do not establish broad population coverage.
- **Overclaiming measurement.** Retrospective reports may blend episodes and reflect visible outcomes; in-the-moment questioning may disrupt absorption. No single behavior, instrument response, or physiological signal here is a direct detector.
