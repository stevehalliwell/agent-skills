## Purpose and locus

Use SDT to investigate **why someone engages, how willingly they engage, and how context supports or obstructs psychological needs**. It distinguishes quality of motivation from amount of effort. Hypothetical comparison: two players persist equally while one enjoys solving and another fears disappointing teammates.

Unit of analysis here: a person doing a specified activity in a specified context and period. Select, for example, choosing a challenge, learning a push rule, retrying a room, or negotiating a cooperative plan. Distinguish willingness to start the game from autonomy during an assigned activity. Within-person differences across sessions are not stable player types; between-person differences are not properties of a mechanic alone.

Useful for questions about meaningful self-direction, intelligible accomplishment, pressure, belonging, and reasons for returning. Not a feature checklist, player taxonomy, clinical diagnosis, retention-maximization recipe, or proof that a design is fun. Do not infer an individual's need satisfaction, capability, mastery, or emotion from authored rules, completion, or play time.

Concept/player experience states broader direction. Individual `aesthetics` records state MDA aesthetic intentions. Neither is a psychological measurement, and SDT needs do not replace MDA aesthetics. An intended feeling of tension can coexist with either willingly accepted challenge or unwanted pressure; SDT helps formulate that distinction, not decide it from a label.

## Organismic model and three needs

SDT starts from an **organismic dialectical** view: people actively explore, develop capacities, and integrate experiences and values into a coherent self. These tendencies are not automatic. They interact with social and cultural conditions that can nourish engagement and integration or foster passivity and defensive responses. This is not a stimulus/reward machine in which any incentive simply adds motivation.

The three basic psychological needs are proposed conditions for healthy functioning, not three preferences some player types possess and others lack. Sources claim universality while allowing different cultural expressions, developmental salience, and routes to satisfaction. That broad claim does not establish what any particular game or player experiences.

### Autonomy: acting with volition

Autonomy means experiencing action as willingly endorsed: it expresses interest or personal value rather than coercion. It is not independence, selfishness, unrestricted action, or option count. A player can willingly accept fixed rules, depend on help, or follow a shared plan. A large menu can still feel controlling if only one option avoids humiliation or disproportionate loss.

Inspect what makes a commitment understandable and endorsable: rationale, consequences, communication, ability to express an approach, and pressure around participation. A constraint can organize meaningful play; its existence alone neither supports nor thwarts autonomy. Choosing to launch a game does not establish volition for every task inside it.

### Competence: experienced effectiveness

Competence concerns feeling effective in interaction with an environment and having opportunities to exercise and develop capacities. Appropriately challenging activity and informative feedback can support it. Difficulty is a task property relative to a person and context, not competence itself. Easy success may feel empty; repeated failure may coexist with meaningful progress, but neither conclusion follows without evidence.

Inspect whether action outcomes are intelligible, whether feedback helps revise an approach, and whether demands allow effective engagement. Actual task success, reported effectiveness, and demonstrated understanding are distinct evidence. Authored skill rank or a `tests_mastery` link is not an acquired capability or competence score.

### Relatedness: connection and mutual regard

Relatedness concerns belonging, connection, and feeling cared for or significant to others. Multiplayer supplies opportunities, not satisfaction: a group may welcome, ignore, or pressure someone. Autonomy and relatedness need not compete; willingly joining a shared commitment can support both.

Solo activity is not evidence of a relatedness deficit. The 2000 account allows intrinsically motivated activity in isolation, with relational security potentially acting as background support. The 2006 game study examines relatedness through other players in MMOs; connection with fictional or computer-generated characters is raised as a question, not established there. Treat a fictional-connection proposal as an untested extension.

### Relationships among needs, context, and motivation

Need support concerns contextual affordances; need satisfaction concerns a person's experience. A proposed supportive rule does not prove satisfaction. Missing support information is not evidence of active thwarting, and low satisfaction is not enough to establish how it arose.

In CET, feeling competent supports intrinsic motivation when accompanied by autonomy. In OIT, relatedness can help a person take in a valued practice, competence can help them participate effectively, and autonomy is especially important for fully integrating its regulation. All three matter to the broader account of wellness. Do not trade them as interchangeable points or total them into a psychological/fun score.

## Motivation and internalization

**Intrinsic motivation:** doing an activity for its inherent satisfaction, interest, or enjoyment. **Extrinsic motivation:** doing it for a separable outcome. Extrinsic does not mean uniformly controlled, shallow, or bad. A personally valued but uninteresting practice can be autonomously regulated.

**Internalization** means taking in a value or regulation. **Integration** means reconciling it with other values and needs so it becomes one's own. Integration does not turn an instrumental activity into intrinsic enjoyment. These distinctions concern reasons for a specified behavior, not labels permanently assigned to people.

Ryan and Deci's 2000 Figure 1 places regulatory forms along relative self-determination. The figure groups four forms under extrinsic motivation and places intrinsic motivation separately at the autonomous end:

- **Amotivation / non-regulation:** lacking intention to act; possible sources include not valuing the activity, not feeling effective, or not expecting action to produce the desired outcome. Perceived causality is impersonal. Stopping play alone does not identify this state.
- **External regulation:** complying with a demand or reward/punishment contingency. Perceived causality is external. Hypothetical example: complete a task only to claim a required prize.
- **Introjected regulation:** internal pressure without full endorsement; avoiding guilt or anxiety, protecting self-worth, or proving worth through performance. Figure labels perceived causality somewhat external; accompanying prose calls it external. Both locate introjection on the controlled side despite its internal pressure. Hypothetical example: continue only because quitting would make oneself feel inadequate. Being internally driven does not make this autonomous.
- **Identified regulation:** consciously accepting the activity's personal importance; perceived causality somewhat internal. Hypothetical example: practise an uninteresting maneuver because it serves a chosen puzzle-solving goal.
- **Integrated regulation:** bringing that accepted regulation into coherence with other values and needs; perceived causality internal. Hypothetical example: sustain practice as part of a personally endorsed commitment to learning and helping one's group. A short observation cannot establish that integration.
- **Intrinsic regulation:** interest and inherent satisfaction, with internal perceived causality. Hypothetical example: experiment with the maneuver because exploring its possibilities is enjoyable in itself.

External and introjected regulation are controlled forms; identified and integrated regulation are more autonomous, alongside intrinsic motivation. These are conceptual distinctions, not a workbench scoring formula. Figure's perceived locus of causality means experienced source of action, not the review's unit of analysis and not physical control of an avatar.

The continuum is **not a required sequence of developmental stages**. People need not pass through each form for a regulation. Different reasons can be relevant to different activities or times; a reward's presence does not identify a player's reason. Do not aim to move every activity into intrinsic motivation or classify every player into one box.

## Six mini-theories and when they matter

Use relevant branch for the selected question, not a compulsory six-part sweep. Current official overview presents six mini-theories; the older papers support some branches in greater depth than others.

### Cognitive Evaluation Theory — CET

Explains contextual changes in intrinsic motivation. Rewards, evaluations, instructions, and feedback can have informational or controlling significance. Feedback that helps someone experience effectiveness differs from feedback that pressures them to prove worth. Autonomy and competence supports are central; relatedness may provide supporting relational context.

Use when an activity already holds interest and a proposal changes feedback, rewards, deadlines, evaluation, or self-direction. Ask what an event communicates, not merely whether a reward exists. The 2000 paper makes strong claims about expected tangible performance-contingent rewards; the 2006 game account also discusses rewards as informational feedback. Preserve contingency and meaning rather than reducing either account to “all rewards destroy fun.” CET cannot explain intrinsic motivation for an activity the person does not find intrinsically interesting.

### Organismic Integration Theory — OIT

Explains extrinsic regulation, internalization, and integration using the distinctions above. Relatedness can make others' valued practices worth considering; competence supports participation; autonomy supports understanding and personally endorsing the practice rather than mere compliance.

Use for tutorials, preparation, group commitments, or other activities pursued for a later outcome. Ask whether a rationale connects to the player's own goal and whether adoption is voluntary. Completing a tutorial proves neither endorsement nor enjoyment; writing a rationale does not prove internalization occurred.

### Causality Orientations Theory — COT

Concerns individual differences in tendencies to interpret environments and regulate action: **autonomy orientation** attends to interest and value; **control orientation** attends to rewards, gains, and approval; **impersonal orientation** involves anxiety concerning competence or amotivation.

Use to keep person–context differences visible when the same event receives different readings. Do not turn this into new player profiles. A single session, choice, or failure does not establish a stable orientation. Current overview supplies these distinctions; full COT methods and validation are outside the captured set.

### Basic Psychological Needs Theory — BPNT

Elaborates the three needs and their relationships with functioning and wellness. All three are essential in this account; supporting one does not cancel costs of thwarting another. It considers variation across cultures and development, not just preference for a game feature.

Use to distinguish “keeps playing” from “experiences willing, effective, connected participation.” Inspect possible costs as well as support. A slice can leave relatedness outside its immediate scope without implying that relatedness is unnecessary to people. Do not derive health or long-term wellness claims from a local design review.

### Goal Contents Theory — GCT

Distinguishes **what goals are pursued**, not just **why action is regulated**. Overview contrasts intrinsic aspirations such as community, close relationships, and personal growth with extrinsic aspirations such as financial success, appearance, and fame. These are theorized to afford need satisfaction differently.

Use cautiously when a design frames what matters, particularly status versus growth or connection. Intrinsic goal content is not intrinsic motivation: someone can pursue community involvement under guilt, or willingly pursue a separable outcome. Source evidence concerns broader aspirations; a fictional treasure goal, currency counter, or cosmetic reward is not automatically an extrinsic life aspiration or a wellness risk.

### Relationships Motivation Theory — RMT

Examines close relationships and group belonging as contexts for need satisfaction. High-quality relationships involve more than contact or relatedness alone: partners support each other's autonomy and, to a lesser degree in the overview's account, competence as well as connection.

Use for questions about cooperative obligations, mentoring, inclusion, or dependence when relationships are actually in scope. Ask whether belonging requires surrendering volition or concealing difficulty. Chat volume and team membership do not establish relationship quality. Current overview is a branch-level account, not direct validation of any specific multiplayer design.

## Game evidence and source versions

The paper by Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski (2006) applies SDT, especially CET and basic-needs theory, through four studies. It develops Player Experience of Need Satisfaction (PENS) measures and studies intuitive interface controls and presence alongside needs. Presence and intuitive controls are **not fourth and fifth basic needs**. Here “controls” refers to the game's input interface; workbench configuration role remains `control-vars`.

- **Study 1:** undergraduates played *Super Mario 64* for 20 minutes. Perceived autonomy and competence related to enjoyment and future-play preference; competence predicted the behavioral choice to continue that game. Not all motivation outcomes had identical predictors.
- **Study 2:** undergraduates played *Zelda: The Ocarina of Time* and *A Bug's Life*, 40 minutes each with randomized order. Need experiences helped account for preference differences. Intuitive controls alone were not enough to explain enjoyment in the less-preferred game.
- **Study 3:** repeated laboratory sessions across four Nintendo 64 games examined within-person and between-person variation. Need experiences helped account for enjoyment/preferences across games; effects differed by outcome and analysis level. This is evidence against treating a player's preference as fixed by one label, not a formula for ranking designs.
- **Study 4:** survey of 730 members of an online community with MMO experience. All three needs independently related to enjoyment and intended future play when considered alongside Yee's achievement, social, and immersion motives. Intended months of future play are not observed future persistence; reported weekly hours are not a direct well-being measure. Incremental prediction in this sample does not make other motivation vocabularies invalid.

Limits matter to application. Studies 1–3 assigned play for course credit rather than relying on ordinary voluntary game selection. Brief sessions and a limited, historical game/interface sample do not establish all-genre effects. Study 4 was self-selected and heavily male; it was a survey, not a randomized test of social features. Measures were new and authors requested further construct validation and attention to prior experience. Relationships and mediation analyses do not establish that any isolated mechanic causes a need experience.

Well-being findings were mixed, not “games improve wellness”: vitality often fell on average, and state self-esteem and mood effects varied. More positive outcomes associated with need satisfaction do not erase those averages. Long-term effects, aggression, and addiction were not tested.

Source versions should remain visible:

- **2000 foundations:** detailed CET/OIT account, continuum Figure 1, internalization, need satisfaction and mental health, and aspiration distinctions. It does not present today's six-branch overview as a complete set.
- **2006 games:** uses abbreviation **BPN** for basic psychological need theory and concentrates on CET/BPN; its relatedness investigation is MMO-specific. Its single-player exclusion of immediate relatedness is a study choice, not repeal of the organismic account.
- **Current captured official overview, undated:** explicitly enumerates **six** mini-theories and uses **BPNT**. Do not claim that all six were tested in 2006 or assign an unsupported date to this expansion. This capture supplies orientation for COT/GCT/RMT, not their full primary research base.

## Inputs and interpretation

Roles: `aesthetics`, `mechanics`, `dynamics`, `challenges`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, challenge routes, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

Read selected concept/mode and bounded rules first. Follow relevant links to interactions, resources, `control-vars`, or `skill-atoms` only when needed to understand the activity. Use existing collection roles; no framework collection, mandatory need profile, or separate measures/decisions/skills collection.

Seek:

- Activity boundaries, intended experience, participation context, and known player assumptions.
- Available actions and commitments, consequences, rule explanation, feedback, assistance, rewards, and social expectations.
- Authored intended/predicted dynamics versus observed behavior, with source/date/context.
- Existing experiment questions, local measures, actual observations, and limitations. An accepted hypothesis with no observations remains unverified.

If only design prose exists, return affordance hypotheses and missing context. If player evidence exists, separate accounts of reasons and experienced effectiveness/connection from behavior such as completion or retry. Neither alone establishes causal support. Do not fill absent information with imagined player stories.

## Apply to one design slice

1. **Bound the question.** Name activity, context, and review purpose. In design mode relate it to stated intent; in explorer mode consider promising leads without inventing a destination. Done: one question such as “Does the energy commitment permit willingly chosen replanning, or mainly enforce compliance?”
2. **Trace authored conditions.** Read exact rules and feedback; distinguish accepted rules, proposals, unknowns, and observations. Done: short fact list with source paths/sections and draft state, without invented geometry or experience.
3. **Choose conceptual distinctions.** Use CET for interest affected by feedback/pressure, OIT for instrumental participation, or another branch only when relevant. Distinguish needs from reasons for acting. Done: explanation of why that branch answers this question; no mandatory category filling.
4. **Build conditional accounts.** Connect a concrete condition to a possible experience, then to a motivation question. State a counterreading and required context. Done: “If the cost is legible and the player endorses the planning goal, limited energy could support meaningful commitment; if consequences are opaque, the same restriction could obstruct effectiveness.” Not “energy creates competence.”
5. **Check evidence and alternatives.** Compare accounts with existing observations without treating absence as refutation. Consider novelty, prior familiarity, reward seeking, obligation, accessibility, and social context where relevant. Done: explicit supported/unsupported distinctions and an observation that could weaken the account.
6. **Return bounded artifact.** Provide finding, source anchors, interpretation, countercheck, and open question, as illustrated below. Keep candidate changes proposed. If an investigation is later authorized, its hypotheses, local measures, method, and observations belong in `experiments`; do not create or execute it during guide application. Done: review usable for discussion, not automatic design adoption or record edits.

## Worked example and review artifact

**Authored facts:** concept uses `game-explorer` mode and offers a candidate direction of thoughtful commitment and making a plan work. Successful pushes require a free destination and enough energy, move the crate one cell, and spend `push-cost`; blocked/insufficient-energy pushes do neither. Pulling is unavailable. Combined-room declares conceptual learning roles, not physical geometry or acquired mastery. Concept assumes single-player interaction and identifies feedback/assistance unknowns. Player story is explicitly hypothetical, not a participant quote.

**Evidence status:** planning experiment has no measures yet, no observations, and has not executed. Route-cost is also unexecuted; its analytical timing reference assumes legal actions and correct decisions, not human puzzle solving. Neither supplies evidence of autonomy, competence, understanding, or motivation.

```text
Scope: committing to a crate push and considering another attempt.
Sources: concept.md / Intended experience and Constraints and risks;
         mechanics/crate-pushing.md / Crate pushing;
         experiments/energy-encourages-planning.md / Observations and Limitations.

Authored trigger: successful pushes consume limited energy; pulling is unavailable.
Interpretation (CET): if consequences are understandable and planning is interesting
  to this player, constrained action could make chosen plans feel effective.
Counterreading: opaque consequences could make the same commitment feel arbitrary;
  knowing the rule still would not prove the player willingly endorses the activity.
OIT distinction: practising to serve a chosen later goal could be identified regulation
  even if practice itself is not enjoyable. No player's regulation is established here.
Evidence: none recorded. Completion or route timing would not resolve these accounts.
Investigation question: what did a player intend, why did they continue or stop,
  and how did they interpret the result of the push?
Potential challenge: a player explains the rule correctly yet reports continuing only
  from obligation. That would weaken a claim of willingly endorsed replanning.
Boundary: hypothetical evidence scenario; no participant, result, or proposal adopted.
```

Relatedness is outside this selected push-rule question, not diagnosed as deficient. **Hypothetical extension:** adding a mentor character might invite a connection question, but the local sources do not establish that this would satisfy relatedness. Do not add multiplayer or fictional relationships merely to fill the third need.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

These are original review prompts, not questionnaire items or validated scales.

- **Endorsement:** what is the player trying to do, and whose reason is it? Could following a fixed route be willingly chosen? Countercheck: many options with a punitive “wrong” choice may provide little felt autonomy.
- **Rule meaning:** does a restriction make a plan legible or only demand obedience? Countercheck: removing it might remove valued structure rather than increase volition.
- **Effectiveness:** what lets a player connect intention, action, and consequence? Countercheck: task success through copying may coexist with little experienced effectiveness; a failed attempt may still inform a chosen revision.
- **Feedback and rewards:** what information does the event give, and what pressure does it impose? Countercheck: a conspicuous prize can draw play without establishing interest in the activity itself.
- **Instrumental activity:** is preparation enjoyable, personally valuable, guilt-driven, or required by an external contingency? Countercheck: persistence does not distinguish these reasons, and several may be relevant.
- **Belonging:** where relationships are in scope, can someone decline a role, ask for help, or leave without losing acceptance? Countercheck: more social interaction can increase pressure rather than connection.
- **Context change:** would the same player interpret the event differently with familiarity, different stakes, or different teammates? Countercheck: do not explain all variation as personality.
- **Disconfirmation:** what evidence would make the preferred support account less plausible? Countercheck: a report of enjoyment alone does not identify which need, rule, or motivation process explains it.

## Common mistakes and transfer limits

- Do not equate autonomy with choice count, competence with difficulty/easy success, or relatedness with multiplayer/contact count.
- Do not equate internal pressure with autonomy, extrinsic regulation with all rewards, or intrinsic aspirations with intrinsic motivation. Goal content, regulatory reason, and need experience answer different questions.
- Do not read the continuum as mandatory stages, a numeric ladder, or a reason to force internalization. Human endorsement is not a design field to set.
- Do not infer need frustration from quitting, need satisfaction from hours played, mastery from challenge links, or emotion from an aesthetic intention.
- Do not make all three needs compulsory features in each slice or treat unknown relatedness as a defect. Broader theory about people is not a requirement that every room satisfy every need directly.
- Do not copy proprietary instruments, invent psychometric thresholds, or sum needs into aggregate psychological/fun scores. This guide is qualitative application support, not PENS administration or a diagnostic tool.
- Review does not authorize record edits, concept revision, feature adoption, experiment preparation/execution, or replacing existing roles. Report uncertainty rather than repair outside scope.
