## Purpose and historical locus

Use this lens to ask what different participants seek in a shared virtual world, how rules enable those interests, and how participants support or displace one another. Its useful result is a situated account of participation tensions, not four labels attached to a feature list.

Bartle's 1996 problem was maintaining a MUD's chosen atmosphere and population relationships. The taxonomy grew from a November 1989–May 1990 discussion among advanced players on one commercial UK MUD: about 15 major contributors and about 15 occasional contributors. They debated what they enjoyed and what changes they wanted. This was not a representative survey of all players or a validated personality instrument. Bartle explicitly warns against expecting conventional psychological rigor.

The article treats MUDs as shared, text-based virtual worlds, including social and game-like variants. Combat permission alone does not distinguish them. A social MUD can contain achievement contests and harmful imposition without implementing combat. Conversely, a combat-capable MUD can sustain a strong social community. Advice about changing population balance is less applicable to social or heavily role-playing MUDs; external commitments may hold those communities together.

**Locus has two levels:**

- At participant level, examine the source of interest in playing this MUD: what someone wants from an activity, not merely which command they use.
- At community level, examine directed effects among approaches over time: whose presence, knowledge, competition, or disruption changes someone else's reasons to stay.

For workbench use, select a bounded activity and affected participants before considering community implications. Do not extrapolate population change from one session. Use the frame for meaningful shared-world participation. For a solo puzzle, a world-oriented comparison may be a loose analogy, but the historical population model does not apply. Report that boundary; do not invent multiplayer features to repair it.

Do not use this guide to diagnose people, allocate universal audience percentages, infer capability or emotion, score fun, or require every design to serve four types.

## Interest graph and four approaches

The 1996 **interest graph** combines two distinctions. The 2012 slide diagram retains the same arrangement, using “action” and “interaction” as endpoint labels.

- **Players / world:** Is the interest chiefly in other human participants or in the virtual environment and its workings? A simulated opponent is part of the world, not automatically a substitute for another person. The target of a command does not by itself settle the source of interest.
- **Acting / interacting:** Is the attraction doing something **to** the target, imposing an intended effect, or doing something **with** it, engaging with what it reveals or how it responds? “Interacting” here does not mean every input/output exchange. “Acting” does not mean every physical action or fast input.

The four approaches occupy quadrants, not four isolated activities:

- **Achievers — acting on the world.** Pursue game-context goals and demonstrable progress, especially points, levels, and formal status in the original MUD. Understanding an obscure rule, asking for advice, or defeating someone can serve advancement rather than be the central attraction. This is not evidence of actual player mastery.
- **Explorers — interacting with the world.** Seek discovery and understanding: first the world's extent, then its underlying rules, unusual responses, and possibilities. Travel is only one means. Repeating an experiment in a familiar room may be more exploratory than visiting a new area along a prescribed route. Points can be an access cost rather than the desired reward.
- **Socialisers — interacting with players.** Seek relationships and knowledge of people through conversation, listening, shared events, and sometimes watching others. The world supplies context for relationships. Playing alongside others or completing a group objective does not alone establish this interest.
- **Killers — acting on players.** Seek to impose themselves on real participants and demonstrate superiority. The 1996 account emphasizes distress and unwanted interference; it also includes rare imposed “help.” Do not soften this into a synonym for all competition, combat, leadership, or PvP. The source distinguishes mutually expected hack-and-slash play from the distress-seeking pattern it describes.

These definitions describe what makes an activity worth doing for someone. The same fight could serve advancement, investigation of a rule, a shared social event, or imposition on another participant. Mechanics alone cannot decide which interpretation fits. Keep plausible alternatives until local evidence supports one.

Bartle describes crossover, leanings, and movement with mood or current style, while also proposing that many players have a main preference and use other approaches instrumentally. Preserve both claims. Do not replace fixed identities with the equally unsupported assertion that preferences never persist. For review, state the activity, context, and period; do not assign a permanent type.

The source permits relative positions and suggests questionnaires or transcript analysis, while noting analysis difficulty. This guide does not turn its axes into computed coordinates or reproduce a typing instrument. A qualitative explanation is sufficient.

## Relationships, populations, and movement

The source enumerates all 16 directed approach-to-approach cases, then combines them into population dynamics. Direction matters: how achievers respond to explorers is not the same question as how explorers respond to achievers. Its stereotyped descriptions are explicitly not claims about an individual.

Important relationships in the source include:

- **Achievement and imposition:** More achievers can attract killers; more killers can drive achievers away. The account also claims that removing too much risk can cheapen achievement for some achievers in these MUDs. That is a historical explanation, not a requirement to permit harassment.
- **Exploration and achievement:** Explorers can reveal routes and methods that speed achievers' advancement. They may also monopolize useful objects. Discovery is neither automatically beneficial nor merely decorative; its effects depend on access, scarcity, and information sharing.
- **Exploration and imposition:** Explorers can share survival knowledge or undermine a killer's reputation. Bartle models a large explorer presence as reducing killers. This is not a mandate to recruit a category of players to perform moderation.
- **Social participation:** Socialisers attract more socialisers when common context helps newcomers enter conversations. Unwanted interference can repel them strongly. More socialisers can also attract killers in the source model, creating indirect negative feedback after initial growth.
- **Different effects of similar numbers:** Achievers can provide events for social conversation without socialisers directly attracting achievers. Explorer numbers have limited direct sensitivity to several other groups, but repeated interruption can still discourage exploration.

Use these as questions about local dependencies, not constants. The article's first-level comparisons omit capacity limits and initially set aside indirect effects. The later dynamics section adds feedback but still excludes many outside influences. Its colored endnote figure encodes increase/decrease and relative effect strength, not fitted coefficients or a predictive simulation.

“Balance” means a relatively stable relationship over time, not equal numbers. Bartle proposes four configurations: killer–achiever equilibrium; socialiser dominance with interference constrained; all groups exerting similar influence despite unequal numbers; and no players. He acknowledges possible other configurations and external stabilizers. These are proposed MUD dynamics, not an exhaustive set of outcomes for modern games or recommended targets.

Rule and administration changes can tilt available interests. Communication access, world size/connectivity, depth of responses, progress rewards, and level structures are source examples. Taking a direction to an extreme can remove the shared-world character itself. Treat these as contextual mechanisms, not a transferable recipe such as “add chat for Socialisers.” A change can help one approach, hinder another, or have delayed effects.

Movement is also part of the model. Participants may move between approaches, and a world supporting several interests may allow that movement without losing its community balance. Ask what permits or blocks a shift: access gates, lost progress, peer expectations, knowledge sharing, or exposure to disruption. Do not invent a mandatory stage sequence or maturation path from these sources.

## 2012 critique and contextual extensions

*A Game Designer's View of Gamification* is an author presentation, not a new validation study. Read its rhetorical examples as an argument about usefulness and purpose, not proof of psychological completeness.

Slides 5–10 offer alternative partitions. The age/body example shows that a partition can be presented as complete yet tell a designer little. Creating/consuming crossed with form/function offers another possible view; freedom/control crossed with aloof/in-your-face is related on slide 9 to solo/group and sandbox/theme-park play. Slide 10 contrasts a Myers-Briggs slice. These are illustrations of how easily attractive quadrant diagrams and labels can be made, not additional Bartle player types to append to the four approaches. The demographic example is not an endorsed audience classification.

The practical extension concerns **activity and reward meaning**. Slides 14 and 16 contrast points with information and describe children wanting stars matching their friends rather than the highest-valued star. A reward's formal rank need not explain its value to a participant. Ask what the reward enables or signifies in this context; do not infer interest from reward collection alone.

Most importantly, slides 15 and 17 recast the contribution: recognizing that MMO players find different things enjoyable matters more than insisting these four categories are the definitive things they enjoy. Slide 18 returns to who the designer wants to engage and leaves a better answer open. This tempers the 1996 taxonomy's broad population claims without supplying a replacement taxonomy.

Workbench adaptation: use the axes to make overlooked interests and conflicts discussable, then test whether the distinctions explain this slice. Do not turn the 2012 points/information contrast into a universal reward law. It motivates a local question; it does not establish what every points collector enjoys or how to change a design.

## Inputs and workbench boundaries

Roles: `mechanics`, `dynamics`, `challenges`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: MDA relationships, experiment evidence, and relevant source records.

Read the selected concept and working mode, relevant participation rules, predicted/observed dynamics, challenge context, and any investigation evidence. Include who can affect whom, access and recovery rules, communication opportunities, and rewards when supplied. Missing population history means population effects stay unknown; it does not prevent a bounded rule-based question.

Keep existing owners. Mechanics own rules; `control-vars` own named configuration; dynamics distinguish intended, predicted, and observed behavior. Experiments own hypotheses, measures, methods, observations, and interpretation. Use the existing 14-role model; no separate measures, decisions, or skills collection and no required framework schema.

Concept/player story expresses overall experience direction. Individual `aesthetics` records express MDA aesthetic intentions, not Bartle types or the entire concept. A declared link to an aesthetic does not prove an interest, feeling, or causal relationship. In game-explorer mode an unknown destination is not a defect.

Guide application returns an interpretation. It does not authorize record edits, experiment execution, feature adoption, or a framework sweep. Save a report only when requested, following the selected review contract.

## Apply to a selected participation slice

1. **Bound the question.** Name the selected session, shared space, activity, or rule change and affected participants. State supplied intent and mode. Check whether human-to-human participation is material. Done: applicability and excluded scope are explicit; stop the population lens when it does not fit.
2. **Reconstruct opportunities and constraints.** Read actual rules: targets, permission to affect others, rewards, information access, loss/recovery, and ways to leave or opt out. Separate authored rules from predictions and observations. Done: each important claim has a source location or is marked unknown.
3. **Interpret interest, not input.** Compare acting/interacting and players/world for relevant activities. Give a reason and an alternative explanation where ambiguous. Done: useful mappings have context and evidence status; no compulsory quadrant filling or permanent player assignment.
4. **Trace a directed tension or support.** Explain who enables or constrains whose activity and by which rule. Consider reverse and indirect effects only where relevant. Done: a plausible mechanism connects participation to consequence; population forecasts remain unverified without suitable evidence.
5. **Check movement and reward meaning.** Ask whether someone can change approach, whether an activity is an end or a means, and whether the same reward has different meanings. Compare with concept intent, not a presumed ideal mix. Done: at least the material alternative explanation or limitation is visible.
6. **Return a bounded finding.** State fact, interpretation, consequence/question, countercheck, and evidence needed. If investigation is useful, suggest it without fabricating measures or results; separately authorized experiments would own those details. Done: user can review the reasoning without accepting a design change.

## Worked examples and output

### Actual tutorial: a justified stopping point

**Authored facts:** The [crate-puzzle concept](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/concept.md), “Platform and context,” assumes single-player interaction. It describes a compact puzzle about crate placement, finite push energy, and a switch opening an exit. Working mode is `game-explorer`. Its player story is explicitly hypothetical, not a participant quote.

**Interpretation:** Comparing a desire to complete the puzzle with a desire to understand its rules might borrow the world-oriented distinction. It cannot classify this player or establish the MUD population relationships. A crate or switch is not another human participant; forceful pushing is not acting on players.

**Evidence:** The [planning investigation](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/energy-encourages-planning.md) has no observations and is not executed. The [route-cost investigation](../examples/crate-puzzle/game-design/experiments/route-cost.md) is also unexecuted; its analytical timing reference is not player evidence. Neither supports an audience-type claim, inferred understanding, or emotion.

**Output:** “Limited applicability: shared-world participation is absent from the selected scope. Stop Bartle population analysis. Do not recommend adding multiplayer or label the candidate audience Achievers.” This preserves the concept rather than treating non-fit as a defect.

### Hypothetical shared-world slice: inspect a tension

This scenario is invented to demonstrate application. It is not a crate-puzzle rule, adopted proposal, or observed session.

**Hypothetical supplied rules:** A persistent shared workshop grants badges for completed machines. Players can publish machine plans, discuss them in a common room, and dismantle any unattended machine, including another player's. Dismantled work requires rebuilding; permission and recovery protections are unspecified.

**Possible interpretations:** Completing machines for badge status could serve achievement. Testing unusual machine behavior and publishing explanations could serve exploration. Discussing collaborators' experiences could serve socialising. Dismantling someone's work might seek imposition, but the command alone cannot establish that: reclaiming parts for one's next badge is an alternative if dismantling returns usable parts, which this scenario has not specified. No actual motive or distress has been observed.

A compact review artifact could read:

> **BARTLE-1 — unverified participation tension; origin: agent.** Scope: hypothetical workshop dismantling rule and plan-sharing activity. Fact within scenario: unattended work can be removed and rebuilding is required. Interpretation: removal may interrupt discovery and shared projects; information sharing may also help badge-seekers advance. Missing permission/recovery rules prevent judging how contained the interruption is. Countercheck: collaborators may have agreed to dismantle test machines, so identical actions could support rather than disrupt discovery. Suggested investigation: establish the permission/recovery contract, then seek contextual accounts of what participants were trying to do before considering a playtest. No observations, population forecast, or adopted rule change. Intent relation: unknown; scenario supplies no concept goal.

This artifact explains one mechanism without filling four category slots, blaming a player type, or prescribing an “optimal” population. If retained through authorized review storage, use real paths/record IDs and revision context rather than invented scenario identifiers as source evidence.

## Questions, counterchecks, and common mistakes

Use questions selectively:

- Would this activity still be attractive if its progress reward vanished? Could discovery or social recognition explain participation instead?
- Is another person the point of the action, a collaborator, an audience, or merely someone occupying the same space?
- Does publishing a discovery open possibilities, remove wanted uncertainty, or expose someone to interference? Whose perspective supports each account?
- Can someone pause achievement to experiment without losing access, status, or their work? What makes returning possible?
- Is imposed “help” welcome? What evidence distinguishes consent from a rule merely permitting the action?
- If a session loses participants, what else changed: availability, server capacity, community commitments, onboarding, or access? Do not attribute departure to a type mix by default.
- Does the category add an explanation beyond restating the command name? If not, keep the concrete activity and drop the label.

Countercheck any proposed tension against actual permissions, agreed competition, recovery costs, session context, and participant accounts. A permitted competitive loss is not automatically distress-seeking; a noncombat interaction is not automatically harmless. Do not infer intent, capability, mastery, or emotion from logs alone.

Common mistakes: treating exploration as map size; treating socialising as chat availability; equating achievement with every completed goal; equating Killers with all PvP; assigning a group objective to Socialisers without asking its purpose; balancing four feature counts; treating low representation as a missing requirement. These replace sources of interest and relationships with surface features.

## Evidence and transfer limits

The article supplies historical observations and a qualitative explanatory model, not measured causal coefficients or a universal population law. Its 16 cases deliberately stereotype approaches; do not carry its derogatory or gendered descriptions into claims about actual participants. External organization, role-play commitments, capacity, and recruitment can change the relationships. Stable proportions do not prove a healthy or desired community.

Useful local evidence would connect activity, stated interest, context, and effects on others over time. A short transcript can show an event, not a permanent identity or retention cause. Distinguish what participants report from what a reviewer infers. Contradictory accounts may reveal different interests or missing context; do not average them into a psychological/fun score.

Both mapped sources were read locally. No “not retrieved” gap remains for the 1996 article or 2012 slides. Scope remains incomplete beyond those works: no later books, expanded typologies, validation literature, presentation audio, captions, or speaker notes were reviewed. Do not import an eight-type model, developmental sequence, or proprietary test from memory.
