## Purpose and locus

Use this lens to explain what information a player must keep available while choosing or carrying out an action, how that information can be recovered, and what learned organization or external support might change the demand. It helps distinguish a remembering problem from a discrimination, rule-understanding, or planning problem before proposing an aid.

Unit of analysis: one bounded decision episode for a specified task and familiarity context. Start when relevant state becomes available; end when the player uses it or no longer needs it. Inspect information, its relations, and access over that interval, not total rules, screen objects, prerequisite records, or whole-game difficulty.

Use for hidden or transient state, delayed instructions, interrupted plans, unfamiliar combinations, repeated state checks, or suspected expert/beginner differences. Do not use to diagnose a person's capacity, infer mastery or emotion, assign a fun score, or prescribe a universal interface limit. Neither seven nor four is a design budget. Memory can be an intended challenge; reducing it is an option, not the lens's goal.

**Source model versus adaptation:** Miller analyzes judgment, immediate recall, and recoding. Cowan argues for a constrained short-term capacity and a possible attentional basis. The decision-episode trace, access descriptions, and design comparison below are workbench adaptations, not published psychological instruments or validated game heuristics.

## Miller: three tasks, not one seven-item law

Miller's article joins several research areas while warning against treating their similar-looking numbers as one mechanism.

- **Absolute judgment:** identify a stimulus by assigning it to a learned category, such as naming a tone's pitch category without a comparison tone. For simple unidimensional stimuli, Miller discusses transmitted information and channel capacity: how reliably responses distinguish alternatives. This is not a count of objects retained in memory. Results vary by attribute; adding dimensions increases distinguishable alternatives, but not by simple addition. Relative comparison and successive judgments change the task.
- **Subitizing versus estimating:** rapid enumeration of small dot sets differs from estimating larger sets. Miller treats numerical resemblance to other spans cautiously, not as proof of one attentional or memory limit.
- **Immediate memory span:** retain several presented items and report them after the sequence. Across the materials reviewed, retained information in bits increases much more than a constant-information account predicts. Miller therefore distinguishes bits (information about uncertain alternatives, not a difficulty score) from **chunks**, familiar units in which the material is organized. His span claim concerns these units, not a fixed number of raw signs or features. Figures 7–8 show the contrast between a constant-information prediction and observed recall.

**Recoding** changes the representation: group elements into a familiar unit that carries more information, then recover its detail when needed. Miller's telegraph example moves from individual sounds to letters, words, and phrases through learning. Table 1 shows binary sequences regrouped and renamed in other codes. But knowing a translation rule is not enough: brief instruction produced smaller gains than expected; slow translation can lose the next group while the previous one is being recoded. Practice matters.

For design, distinguish a readable grouping aid from a learned chunk. A label can suggest organization without making an unfamiliar sequence one retrievable unit. Also ask whether recoding preserves the detail needed: a remembered verbal description can reconstruct or distort details rather than retain an exact copy. Miller's closing discussion explicitly leaves the repeated sevens uncertain.

## Cowan: capacity under specified conditions

Cowan reconsiders what a capacity estimate measures, rather than simply replacing seven with four. He distinguishes:

- **Pure capacity-based estimate:** an estimate in identifiable, separately held chunks, with supplementary contributions sufficiently controlled.
- **Compound estimate:** performance supported by unknown chunk sizes, rehearsal, accessible representations, learned retrieval structures, or repeated use of attention. Compound does not mean bad evidence; such performance matters in real problem solving. It does not isolate the proposed capacity mechanism.

His target article argues that constrained studies converge on group means of about **three to five chunks**, averaging about four. Individual estimates vary more widely. Those estimates do not specify what any player can handle during a game.

### Components and relationships

Cowan's proposed account is a nested-process model, not separate four-slot containers for every sensory channel. Figure 1 depicts the focus within activated memory, with cues favoring which items enter it.

- **Long-term knowledge:** learned concepts and associations make units recognizable and provide retrieval structure. It is involved even in a pure estimate; the aim is not to remove all long-term knowledge.
- **Chunk:** concepts strongly associated with one another and much less strongly associated with other concurrently used chunks. Independence is relative to the task. A word can be a familiar unit in one task while its letters require separate treatment in another.
- **Activated information:** temporarily accessible information, including material within and outside current focus. Cowan allows accessibility through sensory persistence or contextual retrieval, without settling whether time-based decay explains it. Accessibility is not the same as current conscious use.
- **Focus of attention:** the proposed limited-capacity locus where information is available for deliberate report and comparison. Attention can select from activated information, then shift to another set or another level of organization. A long sequence can therefore be recalled without all its detail being in focus at once.
- **Attentional control:** directs selection and switching. Cowan distinguishes control from the focus's contents; they are not interchangeable names for one store.
- **Binding:** retaining which item belongs to which location, order, or current episode can be the critical demand. Remembering that a color appeared is different from remembering where it appeared. Section 4.3.6 and response R2.2.2 clarify that relations, not merely item identities, can drive a task's limit.
- **Maintenance rehearsal:** repeat information to keep it accessible. This can supplement what is held in focus. Recoding and elaborative rehearsal instead emphasize meaningful associations and organization; these processes can overlap.

Cowan's scene-coherence proposal allows several distinct parts of a related scene in awareness. Hierarchical shifting then allows attention to move between a group and its details. This is not evidence that a designer can package any arbitrary set into one scene or chunk. Response R2.2.3 calls important coherence questions open.

### Conditions behind the estimate

The article organizes evidence into four routes. These are research strategies for identifying a capacity limit, not four categories to fill during a design review:

1. **Information overload:** brief arrays or initially unattended material restrict opportunity to rehearse or form larger chunks before testing.
2. **Preventing recoding and rehearsal:** selected procedures constrain mnemonic support, such as serial recall with repeated small stimulus sets and articulatory suppression. Merely adding a second task does not establish that support has been removed.
3. **Performance discontinuities:** changes in accuracy or time as set size grows may indicate recruitment of additional processing. The discontinuity needs explanation; a breakpoint alone does not prove slots.
4. **Indirect effects:** clustering, sequential effects, and model parameters can suggest a limit through their presumed mechanisms. These inferences depend on the model, not just the observed number.

Sections 1.2–1.3 impose further restrictions: units must already be familiar; associations between units must not silently create larger chunks; a still-visible array must not refill attention during report; presentation must allow adequate encoding; and updating or intermediate processing demands must not be mistaken for pure storage. A live puzzle normally permits knowledge, looking back, rehearsal, and reasoning together. Analyze those resources rather than pretending it meets laboratory isolation conditions.

## Disagreements that matter in application

Do not present Cowan's interpretation as settled architecture or summarize every result as support for four.

- **Events and context versus item loss:** Avons, Ward, and Russo argue that order/location precision and contextual distinctiveness can explain limits without a fixed integer set of stored items. Beaman identifies the risk of circular chunk definitions and stresses item-order relations. Cowan's response R2.2 calls for converging empirical identification of chunks and makes binding central. Application: trace *which state belongs where or when*, not just what objects exist.
- **Expertise and long-term retrieval:** Ericsson and Kirk question whether proposed procedures isolate independent chunks, particularly after practice, and whether pure estimates predict everyday skilled performance. Cowan R2.3.4 accepts rich learned retrieval structures but treats them as changing the units and their hierarchical use, not disproving a limit. Application: declare familiarity assumptions and seek evidence for the grouping; neither explanation licenses assigning an expert-sized chunk to a beginner.
- **What limits attention:** Davis reports matched-display evidence against a general four-object attentional limit and proposes interference between binding links. Cowan R2.3.5 questions task demands and possible search/symmetry cues. Application: extra objects may change surface area, similarity, search, or relations as well as memory demand. An object-count comparison alone cannot isolate the cause.
- **Working-memory architecture changes:** Baddeley's commentary distinguishes attentional control from storage and introduces the episodic buffer for integrated temporary representations. Cowan R3.2.1 acknowledges more similarity after this addition and corrects his earlier description of the stores as arbitrary. Do not flatten phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer, and Cowan's focus into equivalent containers.
- **Proposed locus is not proven cause:** response R3.1/Figure R2 distinguishes a limited focus (H1), limited representations (H2), both (H3), and multiple attentional limits (H4). R5 favors H1 but requests cross-domain evidence and leaves mechanism unresolved. Capacity regularity, explanation, and game-design transfer are separate claims.

## Inputs and interpretation

Roles: `skill-atoms`, `challenges`, `mechanics`, `experiments`.

Inspection topics: skill atoms, challenge routes, and relevant source records.

Read selected concept/player experience, relevant rules, challenge and skill-atom links, and any actual presentation or play evidence. Follow related `interactions`, `control-vars`, or other existing owners only when needed to understand the selected episode. Skill prerequisites describe authored relationships, not memory units or demonstrated learning. Configuration values belong in `control-vars`, not copied into a psychological score.

Useful inputs are concrete: what state appears; how long it remains available; where a player can recheck it; what action interrupts access; what changes meanwhile; what relationships must be retained; and what prior practice is actually known. Missing visibility, timing, geometry, or familiarity stays unknown. A rules document alone rarely supplies these conditions.

Compare with the concept's intended experience or, in explorer mode, its candidate direction. Individual MDA aesthetics are not a replacement for that concept. Interpretation can identify a possible memory demand without claiming that demand produces tension, satisfaction, competence, or any other player response.

## Apply to one decision episode

1. **Bound the task.** Name one choice, start/end points, intended reasoning, and supplied familiarity context. Cite source owners and draft state. Done: no whole-game or whole-player capacity claim hides inside the slice.
2. **Trace information access.** At each relevant moment, name the fact or relation needed and whether it is visible, recoverable by rechecking, retained from earlier, or unspecified. Separate current state from predicted future state. Done: the trace explains what must survive which intervening actions.
3. **Separate possible causes.** Is the difficulty recognizing a cue, remembering a vanished state, binding state to its source, applying a rule, updating a plan, or comparing consequences? Several can coexist. Done: a memory interpretation has at least one plausible alternative, not just a count.
4. **Examine organization and support.** Describe possible learned chunks, supporting associations/practice, rehearsal opportunities, and attention shifts. Mark conjectural grouping explicitly. Done: no designer label or prerequisite link is treated as proof of chunking.
5. **Develop one bounded comparison.** Consider a stable cue, history, better relation mapping, staged presentation, or unchanged memory challenge. State what each would externalize and what reasoning it might remove or alter. Done: option remains proposed; nothing is adopted or repaired.
6. **Return a trace and an investigation question.** Include evidence status, alternate explanation, trade-off, and unresolved input. Done: user can inspect the claim without accepting a slot budget or a hidden design change.

Chat or ordinary Markdown is enough. Saving a review or changing records needs separate authority. If an investigation is later authorized, its hypotheses, measures, method, observations, and interpretation belong together in `experiments`; guide application does not execute it or create separate measures, decisions, or skills collections. Existing 14 roles remain unchanged.

**Hypothetical application, not additional game facts:** suppose a candidate presentation shows switch occupancy, then hides the switch area while the player considers a crate push elsewhere. Suppose the review compares that presentation with a persistent occupancy cue. The following is an illustrative review artifact, not a retained record or observed trial:

```text
Memory question: retaining switch occupancy during one later push decision.
Scope: combined-room; from leaving switch view to choosing that push.
Access trace:
- Earlier: occupancy visible in this hypothetical presentation.
- Decision: occupancy hidden; crate destination visible by assumption.
- Needed relation: which switch state belongs to the current plan,
  not merely recognition of the switch icon.
Unknown: geometry, legal push, recheck route, prior practice, delay.
Interpretation: a retained-state demand may compete with plan updating.
Alternative: player might remember occupancy but misunderstand its effect,
  or be unable to reason about the unspecified destination geometry.
Chunk conjecture: a familiar occupancy/exit relation might be retrieved
  together; prerequisite records do not establish that familiarity.
Proposed comparison: persistent current-occupancy cue versus no cue.
Trade-off: cue may remove state recall while leaving route reasoning;
  it may also remove a desired memory challenge or add visual search.
Evidence: none; no capacity estimate or player-experience claim.
Investigation question: does the cue reduce state-confusion episodes
  while preserving the intended need to reason about crate placement?
```

Before treating this as actionable design advice, resolve whether occupancy is actually hidden and whether retaining it is part of the candidate challenge. If occupancy remains visible, shift the question to locating/comparing cues or updating the plan; do not retain the hidden-state diagnosis.

## Practical questions and counterchecks

- Which exact fact must survive a delay, and which *relation* must remain correct? Can a person remember all objects yet swap their states or order?
- Can the player look back, rehearse, or reconstruct the state from a rule? If so, what does this cost in attention, navigation, or disruption to a plan?
- Does the task require simultaneous comparison, or can information be used in sequence? Does that sequence introduce an intermediate result that itself must be retained?
- What makes a supposed chunk meaningful for this player in this context? Would it still be recognized in a new arrangement, or only in a repeated display?
- Does a cue preserve current state, preserve history, explain a rule, or recommend the next move? Those are different interventions.
- Would the same error occur with all relevant state visible? If so, investigate perception, interpretation, or planning before attributing it to recall.
- Could similar icons, stale cues, or a changed state produce interference or context confusion? A persistent cue that lags behind the game can increase errors.
- Does success with support persist when the aid is absent? That would be a separate transfer question, not proof that the aid increased capacity.

For later authorized testing, seek converging evidence: state-specific errors, rechecks, explanation of current state, and performance under a bounded support comparison. Rechecking can be prudent verification, not forgetting. Verbal reports and think-aloud can alter rehearsal or attention; they are not transparent readings of memory. Faster or more accurate play does not by itself establish preserved reasoning or improved experience. Let the experiment own exact measures and their boundaries.

## Common mistakes

- Counting interface elements, control variables, or prerequisite skill atoms as chunks. The unit depends on representation, associations, and task.
- Calling a named combo one chunk for every player. Naming can add another mapping to learn; Miller's recoding example requires practiced translation.
- Assuming visible state has zero cost. Finding, interpreting, and comparing it still require attention; moving information onto screen can change the task rather than merely lighten it.
- Treating slow play, a bad move, or a restart as memory failure. None isolates recall from planning, rule knowledge, motor action, or deliberate exploration.
- Equating four-item regularities with a complete model of working memory, or claiming Miller was simply wrong about seven. Cowan distinguishes conditions and compound performance; the commentaries dispute the mechanism and isolation.
- Removing every memory demand regardless of intent. External support can preserve reasoning, reveal a solution, or eliminate an intended challenge. Keep that trade-off open.
