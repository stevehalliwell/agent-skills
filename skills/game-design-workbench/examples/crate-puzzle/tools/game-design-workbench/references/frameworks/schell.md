## Scope

Reframe a selected design question through concrete player experience, rules, alternatives, and consequences. This is original reflective guidance, not Schell’s deck.

## Inputs and query support

Roles: `mechanics`, `aesthetics`, `challenges`, `experiments`.

Available recipes: `query mda`; `query records --collection <mapped-alias>`.

## Working concepts

- **Contextual question:** A reflective question is useful when it exposes a concrete assumption about an actual player moment. Broad questions that cannot change a decision add little to a bounded review.
- **Perspective and evidence:** Changing perspective can reveal an alternative interpretation without making it true. Keep the new viewpoint tied to source facts and identify what would support or challenge it.
- **Essential relationship:** Ask what a rule contributes and what would change without it. A counterfactual can clarify purpose, but it remains a thought experiment until supported by an appropriate comparison.
- **Consequences and memory:** A local improvement can create costs elsewhere. Retained decisions and rejected alternatives help distinguish a genuinely new option from a renamed version of an old proposal.

## Apply to selected slice

1. Select the exact design question and player moment. Read relevant source, intended experience, and any existing project decisions. State the assumption or choice this reflection should clarify.
2. Describe what the player needs to notice, decide, and experience in that moment. Separate supplied facts from imagined reactions, and mark missing context explicitly.
3. Question one important rule through a counterfactual or contrasting proposal. Explain what the alternative preserves, changes, and costs; do not generate unrelated options for breadth alone.
4. Trace the consequences into adjacent rules, challenges, and retained rationale. Surface the strongest reason against the attractive option rather than treating a fresh perspective as automatic improvement.
5. Return the narrowed question, distinct alternatives, and the assumption worth examining next. These are original reflective prompts, not numbered Schell lens cards or permission to run other frameworks.

## Original prompts

1. What does the player need to notice, decide, and feel at this exact moment?
2. Which rule is essential to that experience, and what would change if it were removed?
3. What attractive alternative serves the same intention through a different constraint?
4. What cost appears elsewhere in the design when this local change succeeds?
5. Which assumption is doing the most work in the current argument?
6. What small comparison could expose that assumption before a durable decision?

## Worked example

- **Authored facts:** Pulling is rejected with rationale, while deliberate placement remains important to the puzzle discussion. A different recovery proposal has not been adopted automatically.
- **Reflection:** Ask what commitment contributes to planning and what would remain if a recovery action were possible. The answer should address an actual decision, not merely declare reversibility good or bad.
- **Competing option:** A bounded recovery action could differ from unrestricted pulling. Compare its rule, cost, and effect against the specific rejection rationale while keeping both alternatives available.
- **Useful next question:** Which assumption matters most: that commitment creates tension, that recovery reduces frustration, or another supplied intention? Name the comparison needed before choosing.

## Evidence and counterchecks

- A thought experiment can expose an assumption but cannot establish an experienced outcome. Label its conclusion accordingly.
- A fair later comparison should address the disputed rule relationship, not reward whichever candidate has more polish or content.
- Consider contrary evidence and any retained rejection rationale. User chooses storage/retention; changed context may justify reconsideration without silently adopting a proposal.

## Common traps

- Do not invent lens-card names, numbers, or attributed wording to give original questions borrowed authority.
- Do not turn a reflective review into an unsolicited sweep of every framework.
- Do not confuse producing more alternatives with resolving the decision that matters.

## Missing data and evidence limits

Start from supplied facts and scoped alternatives. Unknowns become questions/hypotheses, not invented rules. Consult retained alternatives/rationale when available; storage and retention follow user choice.

No copied lens-card wording, numbering, complete deck, or claim of comprehensive book coverage. Do not use this pack as permission to run other framework reviews.
