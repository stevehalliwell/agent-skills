## Scope

Use applicable enjoyment conditions to question a selected mechanic, encounter, or session: concentration, challenge, skills, control, goals, feedback, immersion, and social interaction.

## Inputs and query support

Roles: `mechanics`, `challenges`, `controls`, `experiments`.

Available recipes: `query routes`; `query controls`; `query evidence`; `query records --collection <mapped-alias>`.

## Working concepts

- **Evaluation themes:** GameFlow organizes enjoyment-related questions around eight named elements. Use applicable themes to structure inquiry; this pack supplies original questions, not the published criteria or a scoring reproduction.
- **Control in experience:** Player control concerns understandable action, agency, and response in the selected activity. Workbench control records are tuning parameters; they do not by themselves describe input mappings or experienced control.
- **Goals and feedback:** Distinguish knowing what to attempt, seeing the result, and understanding progress. A visible objective can coexist with feedback that leaves an important rule unclear.
- **Applicability:** Concentration, challenge, skills, control, goals, feedback, immersion, and social interaction are prompts to examine context. Social interaction can be outside scope; omission is not an automatic feature defect.

## Apply to selected slice

1. Select an encounter, mechanic, or bounded session and state its intended experience. Identify which GameFlow themes are relevant to that question instead of conducting an unsolicited whole-game checklist.
2. Read concrete goals, action responses, challenge conditions, and feedback. For each relevant theme, record one source fact or an explicit documentation gap. Avoid judging an interface that source does not describe.
3. Trace a short attempted action from intention to result. Ask where attention, control, or progress could become unclear. Distinguish an unsuccessful strategy from an unexplained input or rule response.
4. Mark inapplicable themes and explain the boundary briefly. Consider whether an apparent interruption or constraint serves an intentional experience before recommending removal.
5. Return a theme-specific question and useful evidence for resolving it. Do not calculate an enjoyment total, reproduce evaluation items, or interpret parameter records as a completed usability review.

## Original prompts

1. What deserves attention now, and what competing presentation could obscure it?
2. Do controls let the player express the intended decision without unexplained loss of control?
3. How are immediate goals and longer-term progress made legible?
4. What feedback distinguishes a misunderstood rule from an unsuccessful strategy?
5. Which elements support sustained involvement, and which interruptions are intentional?
6. If social interaction matters here, what activity enables it? If it does not, why is that criterion inapplicable?

## Worked example

- **Authored facts:** The solo puzzle has blocked-push cases and a switch relationship. It does not include recorded play observations or a complete interface specification.
- **Interpretation:** Blocked-push feedback can be reviewed for whether it explains the relevant condition. Visible switch-state feedback remains a question unless source establishes its presentation.
- **Boundary:** Social interaction is not a missing requirement in this solo scope. This does not prevent discussing immersion or feedback, and it does not require a multiplayer proposal.
- **Useful next question:** Can a player distinguish a blocked target from insufficient energy and understand what action remains available? That is a targeted feedback question, not a global enjoyment judgment.

## Evidence and counterchecks

- Observe attempted actions, perceived responses, and explanations separately. A player recovering after several failures may have learned despite unclear initial feedback.
- Compare intended attention with competing presentation only when presentation evidence exists. A record list cannot establish visual clutter, input latency, or immersion.
- Retain dissenting observations rather than averaging them into a quality score. They may reveal different assumptions or contexts requiring separate interpretation.

## Common traps

- Do not reproduce the published evaluation instrument under slightly altered labels. Keep these questions independently authored and qualitative.
- Do not infer player agency from the existence of adjustable controls such as push-cost.
- Do not treat every theme as mandatory or use social inapplicability as a negative score.

## Missing data and evidence limits

Mark social criteria inapplicable for an intentionally solo experience. Missing goal/feedback prose is a documentation question, not proof players cannot understand the game.

These are original questions, not a reproduction or scoring implementation of the published evaluation instrument. No aggregate enjoyment score. Flow theory and GameFlow are distinct. Control records are adjustable parameters, not a complete account of player agency or input controls.
